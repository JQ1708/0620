Reactivity Series

The chemistry of metals is understood by looking at their reactions. Based on reactions with water and acids, we can create a reactivity series.

This series allows us to place a group of metals in order of reactivity based on observations of their reactions. We also include two important non-metals, Hydrogen and Carbon, because they are used to extract metals from their oxides.

Explaining Reactivity

Reactivity is all about how easily a metal can lose electrons to form a positive ion (a cation). Let's explore this!

What is Oxidation?

(Tap or hover to flip)

Oxidation is the loss of electrons.

What is Reduction?

(Tap or hover to flip)

Reduction is the gain of electrons.

The higher a metal is in the reactivity series:

  • The more reactive it is.
  • The more easily it loses electrons.
  • The more easily it is oxidised.
  • The stronger reducing agent it is.

Displacement Reactions

A more reactive metal can displace (push out) a less reactive metal from its compound because it is a better reducing agent and more easily oxidised.

More Reactive Metal + Less Reactive Metal's Compound → Less Reactive Metal + More Reactive Metal's Compound

e.g., Zinc + Copper Sulfate → Zinc Sulfate + Copper

Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)

In this reaction, Zinc is oxidised (loses electrons) and Copper ions are reduced (gain electrons).

Rusting of Iron

Rusting is a specific type of corrosion that affects iron and steel. It's an oxidation reaction.

The chemical name for rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide.

Conditions for Rusting

Two things are essential for iron to rust:

💧

Water (H2O)

+

💨

Oxygen (O2)

The process of rusting is sped up by the presence of salt or acid rain which act as electrolytes.

Methods of Rust Prevention: Barrier Methods

These methods work by creating a physical barrier to stop oxygen and water from reaching the iron.

  • Painting: Used on cars, ships, and bridges.
  • Oiling / Greasing: Used for moving parts of machinery to prevent rusting and provide lubrication.
  • Coating with Plastic: Used for things like refrigerators, racks, and garden furniture.

Galvanising & Sacrificial Protection

Sometimes, just a barrier isn't enough, especially if it gets scratched. That's where sacrificial protection comes in!

Sacrificial Protection

This involves placing a more reactive metal in contact with the iron. This more reactive metal is a better reducing agent and will corrode (be oxidised) first, "sacrificing" itself to protect the iron.

Magnesium and zinc are often used as they are more reactive than iron.

Example: Protecting a Ship's Hull

  • Blocks of zinc are attached to the steel hull of a ship.
  • Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it loses electrons more easily and is oxidised instead of the steel. The zinc is sacrificed.
  • The zinc blocks don't need to cover the whole hull. As long as they are electrically connected to the steel, they can protect nearby areas.
  • They are often placed on edges and joints where corrosion is most likely.
  • For continued protection, the zinc bars must be replaced before they completely corrode.

Galvanising

Galvanising is a process where iron is coated with a layer of zinc. This can be done by electroplating or dipping it into molten zinc.

This method provides protection in two ways:

  1. Barrier Protection: A layer of zinc carbonate (ZnCO3) forms when zinc reacts with oxygen and carbon dioxide in the air. This layer is unreactive and protects the iron by acting as a barrier.
  2. Sacrificial Protection: If the coating is damaged or scratched, the iron is still protected from rusting by sacrificial protection, as the more reactive zinc corrodes first.

💡 Examiner's Tip

You may be asked to explain why a metal is or is not suitable for preventing rust. Remember that if it is higher in the reactivity series than iron, it will be suitable for sacrificial protection as it will be oxidised instead of iron. If it is lower, it would not be suitable as iron would be oxidised, causing it to rust.
