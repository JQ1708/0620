Separation & Purification

A virtual lab manual for separating mixtures.

Filtration

Use Case: To separate an insoluble solid from a liquid (e.g., sand from water).

Method:

  1. Fold a piece of filter paper into a cone shape.
  2. Place it into a filter funnel.
  3. Pour the mixture through the funnel.
  4. The liquid passes through the tiny pores in the paper, but the solid particles are too large and get trapped.

Residue: The solid left behind in the filter paper.

Filtrate: The liquid that passes through the filter paper.

Crystallisation & Evaporation

Use Case: To separate a soluble solid from a solution (e.g., salt from water).

Method 1: Crystallisation (to form crystals)

  1. Gently heat the solution to evaporate some solvent, creating a saturated solution.
  2. Stop heating when crystals start to form on a glass rod dipped into the solution (the point of crystallisation).
  3. Allow the solution to cool slowly. As it cools, the solid becomes less soluble and crystals form.
  4. Filter the crystals from the remaining solution.
  5. Wash the crystals with a small amount of cold distilled water and dry them.

Method 2: Evaporation (to get a dry solid)

  1. Pour the solution into an evaporating dish.
  2. Heat until all the solvent has evaporated, leaving the solid behind.

Note: This is faster but should only be used if the solid is thermally stable (doesn't decompose when heated).

Distillation

Principle: Separating substances based on differences in their boiling points.

Simple Distillation

Use Case: Separating a liquid solvent from a solution (e.g., pure water from salt water).

Method:

  1. The solution is heated and the liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates, forming a vapor.
  2. The vapor rises and enters the condenser.
  3. The condenser is kept cold by circulating water, causing the vapor to cool and turn back into a liquid.
  4. The pure liquid (distillate) is collected in a beaker.
Safety Note

Add boiling chips for smooth boiling. Do not fill the flask more than halfway.

Fractional Distillation

Use Case: Separating a mixture of miscible liquids with different boiling points (e.g., ethanol and water, or separating crude oil).

Method:

  1. The mixture is heated. The liquid with the lowest boiling point evaporates first.
  2. The vapor rises through a fractionating column, filled with glass beads to provide a large surface area.
  3. The column has a temperature gradient (hottest at the bottom, coolest at the top).
  4. The vapor of the substance with the lower boiling point will reach the top of the column first, then pass into the condenser.
  5. Vapors of liquids with higher boiling points will condense on the large surface area of the beads and fall back down.

Paper Chromatography

Use Case: To separate and identify dissolved substances from a mixture (e.g., different colored inks).

Method:

  1. Draw a baseline on the chromatography paper in pencil (pencil is insoluble and won't move).
  2. Spot the mixture onto the baseline.
  3. Place the paper in a beaker with a solvent, ensuring the solvent level is below the baseline.
  4. The solvent moves up the paper, dissolving the mixture and carrying it up the paper. The resulting paper is called a chromatogram.
  5. Substances that are more soluble in the solvent and have weaker attractions to the paper travel further.

Rf Values & Locating Agents

For colorless substances (like amino acids), a locating agent is sprayed on the chromatogram to make the spots visible. The position of a spot is identified by its Rf value:

Rf = Distance moved by the substanceDistance moved by the solvent front

  • The Rf value of a particular compound is always the same (under the same conditions).
  • This allows substances to be identified by comparing their Rf values to known values from a database.
  • The Rf value is always less than 1.

Assessing Purity

Pure substances can be distinguished from mixtures by their melting and boiling points. This method is often used to assess the purity of drugs.

Pure Substances

Have a sharp, fixed melting point and a fixed boiling point (e.g., pure water boils at exactly 100°C).

Mixtures

Melt and boil over a range of temperatures.

Effect of Impurities: Impurities tend to lower the melting point and increase the boiling point of a substance.
