Group I: The Alkali Metals

These metals are highly reactive because they all have one electron in their outer shell which is easily lost to form a +1 ion.

Physical Properties

  • They are soft and easy to cut.
  • They have low densities (Li, Na and K float on water).
  • They have low melting and boiling points that decrease down the group.

Chemical Properties & Trends

Alkali metals react vigorously with water to produce a metal hydroxide (an alkaline solution) and hydrogen gas. Reactivity increases dramatically down the group.

MetalObservation with Water
Lithium (Li)Fizzes steadily on the surface.
Sodium (Na)Melts into a ball, fizzes rapidly, and darts across the surface.
Potassium (K)Instantly ignites, burns with a lilac flame, and fizzes violently.

Why does reactivity increase down the group?

As you go down the group, the outer electron is further from the nucleus, has more shielding from inner shells, and is less strongly attracted. This means it is lost more easily.

Group VII: The Halogens

These non-metals are reactive because they all have seven electrons in their outer shell and need to gain one electron to get a full outer shell. They exist as diatomic molecules (e.g., Cl₂).

Physical Trends

As you go down the group, the melting/boiling points and density increase, and the colours get darker.

HalogenColour & State at Room Temp
Fluorine (F₂)Pale yellow gas
Chlorine (Cl₂)Pale green gas
Bromine (Br₂)Red-brown liquid
Iodine (I₂)Grey-black solid

Chemical Properties & Trends

A more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halide from an aqueous solution of its salt. Reactivity decreases down the group.

ReactionObservation
Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)Solution turns orange-brown as bromine is displaced.
Br₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → 2KBr(aq) + I₂(aq)Solution turns brown as iodine is displaced.

Why does reactivity decrease down the group?

As you go down the group, the outer shell is further from the nucleus and has more shielding from inner shells. This makes it harder to attract and gain an electron.

The Transition Metals

These elements are found between Group II and Group III. They have properties that are very different from the alkali metals.

PropertyTransition MetalsGroup I Metals
HardnessHard and strongSoft
Melting PointHighLow
DensityHighLow

Special Catalytic & Chemical Properties

Transition metals have three special properties that other metals do not:

Form Coloured Compounds

e.g., Copper(II) sulfate is blue, Potassium Manganate(VII) is purple.

Act as Catalysts

e.g., Iron in the Haber process, Nickel in hydrogenation.

Variable Oxidation States

e.g., Iron can form Fe²⁺ (green) and Fe³⁺ (red-brown) ions.

Group 0: The Noble Gases

These elements are extremely unreactive (inert) because they have full outer electron shells, which is a very stable configuration.

Properties & Trends

  • They are all colourless, monatomic gases at room temperature.
  • They are unreactive and do not form bonds easily.
  • The boiling points and densities increase as you go down the group.
  • Electronic Configurations: He (2), Ne (2,8), Ar (2,8,8).

Uses

  • Their inertness makes them useful for providing a non-reactive atmosphere.
  • Helium: Used in balloons and airships due to its low density and non-flammability.
  • Argon: Used inside filament light bulbs to stop the filament from burning away.
