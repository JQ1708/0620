Question 1

Topics: Chemical Processes & Separation Techniques

Name the process used to:

(a) produce ammonia from nitrogen [1]

The Haber (process).

Recap

The Haber process is the industrial process used to make ammonia from nitrogen (from the air) and hydrogen (from methane). It uses an iron catalyst, a high temperature of 450 °C, and a very high pressure of 20 000 kPa (200 atm).

(b) produce lead from molten lead(II) bromide [1]

Electrolysis.

Recap

Electrolysis is the decomposition (breakdown) of an ionic compound, when molten or in an aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current. The substance being decomposed is the electrolyte.

(c) separate an insoluble solid from a mixture of an insoluble solid and a solution [1]

Filtration.

Recap

Filtration is used to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid. The solid which remains on the filter paper is called the residue. The liquid or solution that passes through the filter paper is called the filtrate.

(d) produce ethanol from ethene [1]

Catalytic addition (of steam).

Recap

Alkenes, like ethene, undergo addition reactions. Ethanol is manufactured by the catalytic addition of steam (water) to ethene. The conditions are a temperature of 300 °C, a high pressure of 6000 kPa (or 60 atm), and an acid catalyst (e.g., phosphoric(V) acid).

(e) identify the components of a mixture of soluble coloured substances [1]

Chromatography (specifically, paper chromatography).

Recap

Paper chromatography is a technique used to separate and identify a mixture of soluble coloured substances. It works because different substances travel at different speeds up the paper as the solvent moves. The paper with the separated spots is called a chromatogram.

(f) separate a mixture of several liquids with different boiling points [1]

Fractional distillation.

Recap

Fractional distillation is used to separate a mixture of liquids with boiling points which are close to each other (e.g., separating petroleum fractions or ethanol from water). Simple distillation is used when liquids have very different boiling points (e.g., separating pure water from a salt solution).

(g) determine the volume of an acid required to neutralise a given volume of an alkali. [1]

Titration.

Recap

An acid-base titration is a method used to find the concentration of an acid or alkali. It involves using a burette to add one solution to a known volume of the other solution (measured with a volumetric pipette) in a flask. An indicator is used to show when the end point (neutralisation) is reached.

Recap

  • Proton number (atomic number) is the number of protons in the nucleus. It identifies the element (e.g., 29 is Cu, 17 is Cl, 30 is Zn).
  • Mass number (nucleon number) is the total number of protons and neutrons.
  • Number of neutrons = Mass number - Proton number. (For Cu: 63 - 29 = 34).
  • In a neutral atom (like 63Cu), the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons (29).
  • In an ion, the number of electrons is different.
    • A negative ion (anion) like Cl⁻ has gained electrons. (Cl has 17 protons, so Cl⁻ has 17 + 1 = 18 electrons).
    • A positive ion (cation) like the one with 30 protons and 28 electrons has lost 2 electrons, so it is Zn²⁺.

(i) Explain why graphite conducts electricity when solid. [1]

Graphite conducts electricity because it has delocalised or mobile electrons that are free to move along its layers and carry charge.

Recap

Graphite is a giant covalent structure arranged in layers of carbon atoms. Each carbon atom forms three strong covalent bonds. The fourth outer electron from each carbon atom is delocalised (not associated with any particular atom) and is free to move, allowing graphite to conduct electricity.

(ii) Complete the dot-and-cross diagram in Fig. 3.1 of a molecule of oxygen. Show outer shell electrons only. [2]

A double covalent bond is formed between the two oxygen atoms. Each oxygen atom shares two pairs of electrons (4 electrons in total, shown as dots and crosses in the overlap). Each oxygen atom also has 4 non-bonding electrons (2 lone pairs) in its outer shell to achieve a stable octet.

Recap

A covalent bond is a pair of electrons shared between two atoms. Oxygen is in Group VI, so it has 6 outer electrons. To get a stable shell of 8, it must form two covalent bonds. It does this by forming a double covalent bond (O=O) with another oxygen atom, sharing two pairs of electrons.

(iii) Deduce the physical state of carbon monoxide at -195 °C. Use the data in Table 3.1 to explain your answer. [2]

Physical state: Liquid

Explanation: The temperature of -195 °C is below the boiling point (–191 °C) but above the melting point (–199 °C).

Recap

A substance is a solid at temperatures below its melting point. It is a liquid at temperatures between its melting point and boiling point. It is a gas at temperatures above its boiling point.

(iv) Explain in terms of structure and bonding why graphite has a much higher melting point than carbon monoxide. [3]

Graphite has a giant covalent structure. A very large amount of energy is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds between all its carbon atoms.

Carbon monoxide has a simple molecular structure. While it has strong covalent bonds *within* each molecule, the forces *between* the molecules (intermolecular forces) are weak. Only a small amount of energy is needed to overcome these weak forces.

Recap

Substances with giant structures (ionic, metallic, or covalent) have high melting and boiling points because strong bonds must be broken throughout the lattice.

Substances with simple molecular structures (like O₂ or CO) have low melting and boiling points because only weak intermolecular forces (not the strong covalent bonds) need to be overcome to separate the molecules.

(b) Potassium reacts with chlorine to form potassium chloride. Write a symbol equation for this reaction. [2]

2K + Cl₂ → 2KCl

Recap

Potassium (K) is a Group I metal and chlorine (Cl₂) is a Group VII halogen (which exists as a diatomic molecule). A metal and a non-metal react to form an ionic compound. Potassium forms a K⁺ ion and chlorine forms a Cl⁻ ion, so the formula of the product is KCl. The equation must be balanced to have 2 K and 2 Cl on both sides.

(c) A dilute aqueous solution of potassium chloride undergoes electrolysis. Oxygen is produced at the anode.

(i) State what is meant by the term electrolysis. [2]

Electrolysis is the breakdown (decomposition) of an ionic compound, when molten or in an aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

Recap

The electrolyte is the substance that is broken down and conducts electricity. The anode is the positive electrode and the cathode is the negative electrode. Conduction in the electrolyte is due to the movement of ions, while conduction in the wires is by electrons.

(ii) Write an ionic half-equation for the production of oxygen at the anode. [2]

4OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) + 4e⁻

Recap

In the electrolysis of dilute aqueous solutions, water provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions. At the anode (positive electrode), negative ions (Cl⁻ and OH⁻) are attracted. Because the potassium chloride solution is dilute, hydroxide ions (OH⁻) from the water are discharged in preference to chloride ions. Oxidation (loss of electrons) occurs at the anode.

Topics: Reversible Reactions, Equilibrium, Oxidation Numbers

Dinitrogen tetroxide, N₂O₄, decomposes into nitrogen dioxide, NO₂. The reaction is reversible.

N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g)
colourless gas      brown gas

Fig. 4.1 shows a gas syringe containing a mixture of dinitrogen tetroxide and nitrogen dioxide. The gas syringe is sealed. The mixture reaches equilibrium and the colour of the mixture of gases is a pale brown.

(a) Describe a reversible reaction at equilibrium in terms of:
the rate of the forward reaction and the rate of the reverse reaction [2]
the concentration of reactants and products.

Rate: The rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the reverse reaction.

Concentration: The concentrations of all reactants and products remain constant (they are no longer changing).

Recap

An equilibrium reaction is one where reactants are converted to products at the same rate as products are converted back to reactants. This dynamic state can only be reached in a closed system. At equilibrium, the macroscopic properties (like colour and concentration) are constant.

(b) The pressure of the mixture is increased. All other conditions stay the same. The mixture immediately turns darker brown before the position of equilibrium changes. Explain in terms of particles why the mixture immediately turns darker brown. [1]

Increasing the pressure (by pushing the plunger in) decreases the volume. This means there are more brown nitrogen dioxide molecules per unit volume, so the concentration of the brown gas instantly increases, making the colour appear darker.

Recap

The pressure of a gas is related to the concentration of its particles. Decreasing the volume forces the same number of particles into a smaller space, increasing the concentration of all gaseous species present.

(c) The temperature of the mixture is increased. All other conditions stay the same. The mixture turns darker brown. State what can be deduced about the forward reaction from this information. [1]

The forward reaction is endothermic.

Recap

Le Chatelier's principle states that if a change is made to a system at equilibrium, the system will respond to oppose the change.
When temperature is increased, the equilibrium shifts in the direction of the endothermic (heat-absorbing) reaction to use up the added heat. Since the mixture turned darker brown (more $NO_{2}$), the equilibrium shifted to the right (the forward reaction). Therefore, the forward reaction must be endothermic (ΔH is positive).

(d) Sulfur is converted into sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, by a series of reactions. Sulfur dioxide, SO₂, and oxygen, O₂, react to form sulfur trioxide, SO₃. The reversible reaction reaches equilibrium.

Recap

Effect on Rate:

(a) Barium sulfate, BaSO₄, is an insoluble salt and is made by precipitation.

(i) Name two aqueous solutions that produce a precipitate of barium sulfate when they are mixed. [2]

1. A soluble barium salt, e.g., barium chloride or barium nitrate.

2. A soluble sulfate, e.g., sodium sulfate, potassium sulfate, or sulfuric acid.

Recap

To make an insoluble salt by precipitation, you must mix two soluble solutions. One solution must contain the positive ion (e.g., Ba²⁺ from soluble $BaCl_{2}$) and the other must contain the negative ion (e.g., SO₄²⁻ from soluble $Na_{2}SO_{4}$).

Solubility Rules: All nitrates are soluble. Most chlorides are soluble (except lead, silver). Most sulfates are soluble (except barium, lead, calcium). All Group I (e.g., sodium, potassium) salts are soluble.

(ii) Describe how to produce a pure sample of barium sulfate from the mixture of aqueous solutions in (a)(i). [2]

1. Filter the mixture. The solid barium sulfate (the residue) will be trapped by the filter paper.

2. Wash the residue on the filter paper with distilled water to remove any dissolved impurities.

3. Dry the solid, for example by leaving it in a warm oven or pressing it between filter papers.

Recap

The preparation of an insoluble salt by precipitation involves three key steps after mixing the solutions: filtration to separate the solid, washing to remove impurities, and drying to get the pure solid salt.

(iii) Write an ionic equation for the precipitation reaction which produces barium sulfate. Include state symbols. [3]

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

Recap

An ionic equation shows only the species that change during the reaction. The soluble starting salts (e.g., $BaCl_{2}$ and $Na_{2}SO_{4}$) are split into their ions: Ba²⁺(aq), 2Cl⁻(aq), 2Na⁺(aq), and SO₄²⁻(aq). The ions that do not form the precipitate (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) are spectator ions and are omitted from the final equation.

(b) Soluble salts are made from dilute acids. Name the dilute acid and one other substance that react together to make copper(II) sulfate. [2]

Dilute acid: Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄)

Other substance: Copper(II) oxide (or copper(II) carbonate, or copper(II) hydroxide)

Recap

To make a soluble salt from an insoluble base (like copper(II) oxide) or carbonate, you add the excess solid base to the acid and warm it. The unreacted solid is then filtered off, leaving a solution of the salt (copper(II) sulfate), which can then be crystallised. (Note: Copper metal is not reactive enough to react with dilute acids).

(ii) In an experiment, 0.574 g of ZnSO₄·xH₂O is heated until the mass is constant. The mass of ZnSO₄ that remains is 0.322g. [Mᵣ: ZnSO₄, 161; H₂O, 18] Determine the value of x using the following steps. [4]

Calculate the number of moles of ZnSO₄ remaining.

Moles = mass / $M_{r}$ = 0.322 g / 161 g/mol = 0.00200 mol

Calculate the mass of H₂O given off.

Mass = (Mass of hydrated salt) - (Mass of anhydrous salt)
Mass = 0.574 g - 0.322 g = 0.252 g

Moles = mass / $M_{r}$ = 0.252 g / 18 g/mol = 0.0140 mol

Topics: Metal Extraction (Blast Furnace), Transition Metals, Rusting

(a) Fig. 6.1 shows a blast furnace used to extract iron from its ore.

(i) Name the main ore of iron used in the blast furnace. [1]

Hematite (which is mainly iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃).

(ii) Name the substance that enters the blast furnace at A. [1]

Hot air (which provides the oxygen for the combustion of coke).

(iii) Name the reducing agent in the extraction of iron in the blast furnace. [1]

Carbon monoxide (CO).

(iv) Explain why limestone is added to the blast furnace. Give details of the chemical reactions that are involved. [3]

Limestone (calcium carbonate, $CaCO_{3}$) is added to remove acidic impurities from the iron ore, such as silicon(IV) oxide ($SiO_{2}$).

1. First, the limestone is broken down by the high heat in a thermal decomposition reaction:
$CaCO_{3}(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_{2}(g)$

2. The calcium oxide (CaO) produced is a basic oxide. It reacts with the acidic impurity $SiO_{2}$ to form a molten slag (calcium silicate), which is tapped off separately from the iron.
$CaO(s) + SiO_{2}(s) \rightarrow CaSiO_{3}(l)$

Recap

In the blast furnace, limestone (calcium carbonate) undergoes thermal decomposition to form calcium oxide (a base). This base neutralises the acidic silicon(IV) oxide impurity (sand) found in the iron ore. This reaction forms molten slag (calcium silicate), which floats on top of the molten iron and is removed.

(b) The list shows the properties of some elements. ... State which property from the list: [4]

• act as catalysts
• have low densities
• have low melting points
• form acidic or basic oxides
• form coloured compounds
• form positive or negative ions

(i) is true for sodium but not iron

Have low densities (or have low melting points). Sodium (a Group I metal) is a soft, low-density metal, while iron (a transition element) is dense and has a high melting point.

(ii) is true for iron but not sodium

Act as catalysts (or form coloured compounds). These are characteristic properties of transition elements like iron. Group I metals like sodium do not share these properties.

(iv) is not true for sodium and not true for iron.

Form... negative ions (or form acidic oxides). These are characteristic properties of non-metals.

Group I Metals (e.g., Sodium):

Transition Elements (e.g., Iron):
• Hard, high density, high melting points.
• Form coloured compounds.
• Often act as catalysts.
• Have variable oxidation states (e.g., Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺).

(c) Steel consists mainly of iron. Iron rusts when it reacts with water and oxygen. Fig. 6.2 shows magnesium blocks attached to the bottom of a steel boat. The magnesium does not completely cover the steel. The magnesium blocks provide sacrificial protection for the steel.

(i) Explain, in terms of electrons, why magnesium is used for sacrificial protection. [2]

Magnesium is more reactive than iron. This means magnesium loses electrons more readily (is more easily oxidised) than iron is. Magnesium therefore corrodes in preference to the iron, "sacrificing" itself. It donates its electrons to the iron, forcing the iron to remain as atoms and thus preventing it from rusting (forming Fe³⁺ ions).

Recap

Sacrificial protection is a method of rust prevention. It works by attaching a more reactive metal (like zinc or magnesium) to the iron. The more reactive metal loses electrons (is oxidised) more easily and corrodes first. Galvanising is an example that uses zinc, providing both a barrier and sacrificial protection.

(ii) Name a metal that cannot provide sacrificial protection for steel. [1]

Copper (or silver, gold, lead, or any metal that is below iron in the reactivity series).

(a) (i) An organic compound A has the following composition by mass.
C, 83.33%; Η, 16.67%
Calculate the empirical formula of compound A. [3]

Step 1: Divide percentage by $A_{r}$ to find mole ratio.
C: 83.33 / 12 = 6.944
H: 16.67 / 1 = 16.67

Step 2: Divide by the smallest number to get simplest ratio.
C: 6.944 / 6.944 = 1
H: 16.67 / 6.944 = 2.4

Step 3: Convert to simplest whole number ratio.
Multiply by 5 to get whole numbers: C: 1 × 5 = 5
H: 2.4 × 5 = 12

Empirical formula = C₅H₁₂

Recap

The empirical formula is the simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms in a compound. It is calculated by dividing the mass (or percentage mass) of each element by its relative atomic mass ($A_{r}$), and then finding the simplest whole number ratio of these molar amounts.

(ii) Compound B has the empirical formula C₂H₅ and a relative molecular mass ($M_{r}$) of 58. Determine the molecular formula of compound B. [2]

Step 1: Find the mass of the empirical formula.
Mass of C₂H₅ = (2 × 12) + (5 × 1) = 29

Step 2: Divide the $M_{r}$ by the empirical mass to find 'n'.
n = (Relative Molecular Mass) / (Empirical Formula Mass)
n = 58 / 29 = 2

Step 3: Multiply the empirical formula by n.
Molecular formula = (C₂H₅) × 2 = C₄H₁₀

Recap

The molecular formula shows the actual number of each type of atom in one molecule. It is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. This multiple (n) is found by dividing the relative molecular mass ($M_{r}$) by the mass of the empirical formula.

(b) (i) Identify the functional group in alkenes that reacts when alkenes form polymers. [1]

The C=C double bond (carbon-carbon double bond).

Recap

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons. Their functional group is the C=C double bond. This is the site of their characteristic reactions, addition reactions, including addition polymerisation, addition of bromine, and addition of hydrogen (hydrogenation).
