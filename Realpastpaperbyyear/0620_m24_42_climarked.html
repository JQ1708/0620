Question 1

Paper: 0620/42/F/M/24
Topics: Metals and reactivity, Extraction of iron

Iron ore contains iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃. A blast furnace is used to extract iron from Fe₂O₃.

Equations for some of the reactions in the blast furnace are shown.

equation 1     C + O₂ → CO₂

equation 2     CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

equation 3     CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃

(a) (i) Name the substance which provides the carbon for this reaction. [1]

The substance which provides the carbon for this reaction is coke.

Recap

The raw materials for the extraction of iron in the blast furnace are hematite ore, limestone and air. Carbon monoxide is formed in the blast furnace by the reaction of coke (carbon) with carbon dioxide.

(a) (ii) State the purpose of the combustion of carbon in the blast furnace. [1]

The purpose of the combustion of carbon is to provide heat for the blast furnace, as it is a highly exothermic reaction.

Recap

Combustion is an exothermic chemical reaction in which a fuel and an oxidising agent (usually oxygen) react. Exothermic reactions release thermal energy to the surroundings.

(b) (i) Calculate the percentage by mass of iron in iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃. [2]

1. First, calculate the relative formula mass (Mr) of Fe₂O₃.
Mr(Fe₂O₃) = (2 × Ar(Fe)) + (3 × Ar(O)) = (2 × 56) + (3 × 16) = 112 + 48 = 160.

2. Next, find the total mass of iron in the formula: 2 × 56 = 112.

3. Finally, calculate the percentage: (Mass of Fe / Mr of Fe₂O₃) × 100 = (112 / 160) × 100 = 70.0%.

Recap

Percentage composition by mass is calculated using the formula:
Percentage composition by mass = mass of a particular element in a mole of a compoundmass of one mole of the compound × 100.

(b) (ii) State the name of the iron ore which consists mainly of iron(III) oxide. [1]

The iron ore which consists mainly of iron(III) oxide is hematite.

Recap

An ore is a rock containing a compound from which a metal is extracted. Hematite is an ore of iron which contains iron(III) oxide. Bauxite is an ore of aluminium.

(b) (iii) Describe how carbon monoxide is formed in the blast furnace. [1]

Carbon monoxide is formed in the blast furnace by the reduction of carbon dioxide by hot coke (carbon). The equation is CO₂ + C → 2CO.

Recap

Carbon monoxide is formed in the blast furnace by the reaction of coke (carbon) with carbon dioxide. Carbon monoxide then reduces iron(III) oxide to iron.

(b) (iv) Write the symbol equation to show the reaction that occurs when iron(III) oxide is converted to iron in the blast furnace. [2]

The symbol equation is: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.

Recap

This is a redox reaction. The iron(III) oxide is reduced (loses oxygen) to iron, and the carbon monoxide is oxidised (gains oxygen) to carbon dioxide.

(b) (v) Name the chemical process which happens to iron when iron(III) oxide is converted to iron in the blast furnace. [1]

The chemical process is reduction.

Recap

Reduction is loss of oxygen from a compound. Oxidation is gain of oxygen. In this case, iron(III) oxide loses oxygen to become iron.

(c) State the type of reaction shown by equation 2. [1]
(CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)

This reaction is thermal decomposition.

Recap

Thermal decomposition is the breakdown of a substance by heating strongly. The thermal decomposition of limestone (calcium carbonate) in the blast furnace produces calcium oxide and carbon dioxide.

(d) (i) Explain why the reaction in equation 3 can be described as an acid-base reaction. [2]
(CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃)

This is an acid-base reaction because calcium oxide (CaO) is a basic oxide and silicon(IV) oxide (SiO₂) is an acidic oxide.

Recap

Basic oxides react with acids. They are oxides of metals. Acidic oxides react with bases. They are oxides of non-metals. Amphoteric oxides, like zinc oxide, react with both acids and bases.

(d) (ii) State:

  • the chemical name of SiO₂
  • the common name given to CaSiO₃ when it is formed in the blast furnace.
[2]

The chemical name of SiO₂ is silicon(IV) oxide.
The common name for CaSiO₃ is slag.

Recap

Calcium oxide (a base) reacts with acidic impurities in the iron ore, such as silicon(IV) oxide, to form molten slag (calcium silicate). This slag floats on top of the molten iron and is removed.

(e) (i) State why aluminium is not extracted from its ore using a blast furnace. [1]

Aluminium is not extracted using a blast furnace because aluminium is more reactive than carbon (it is above carbon in the reactivity series), so carbon cannot reduce aluminium oxide.

Recap

Metal oxides above carbon in the reactivity series are extracted by electrolysis. Metal oxides below carbon in the reactivity series can be extracted by reduction with carbon or carbon monoxide when heated.

(e) (ii) Name the process used to extract aluminium from its ore. [1]

The process used to extract aluminium is electrolysis.

Recap

The main ore of aluminium is bauxite, which contains aluminium oxide. Because aluminium is a reactive metal, electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide (dissolved in cryolite) is required to extract it.

(f) (i) Both iron(III) oxide and aluminium oxide contain metal ions with a 3+ charge.
Write the electronic configuration of an Al³⁺ ion. [1]

An aluminium atom (proton number 13) has the electronic configuration 2,8,3. To form an Al³⁺ ion, it loses 3 outer shell electrons, so its electronic configuration becomes 2,8.

Recap

Positive ions are formed when atoms lose one or more electrons. Negative ions are formed when atoms gain one or more electrons. The resulting electronic configuration is often that of the nearest noble gas, which is very stable.

(f) (ii) Deduce the number of protons and electrons in an Fe³⁺ ion. [2]

An iron (Fe) atom has a proton number of 26, which means it has 26 protons.
An uncharged atom would also have 26 electrons. To form an Fe³⁺ ion, the atom loses 3 electrons, leaving it with 26 - 3 = 23 electrons.

Recap

The proton number (or atomic number) is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. In an uncharged atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons. An ion is formed when an atom gains or loses electrons.

Topics: The Periodic Table, Group VII Halogens, Bonding

The elements in Group VII of the Periodic Table are known as the halogens. Halogens can form halide ions.

(a) Identify the halogen with the lowest density at r.t.p. (room temperature and pressure). [1]

The halogen with the lowest density at r.t.p. is fluorine.

Recap

There is a trend in the physical properties of Group VII elements. As you go down the group, the melting point, boiling point, and density all increase. Fluorine is at the top, so it has the lowest density.

(b) State the appearance of bromine at r.t.p. [1]

At r.t.p., bromine is a red-brown liquid.

Recap

At r.t.p. chlorine is a yellow-green gas, bromine is a red-brown liquid and iodine is a grey-black solid. The states change from gas to liquid to solid as you go down the group.

(c) Use the Periodic Table to:

  • give the symbol of the halogen with the highest atomic number
  • deduce the number of occupied electron shells in an atom of this element.
[2]

The halogen with the highest atomic number is Tennessine (Ts).
It is in Period 7, which means an atom of this element has 7 occupied electron shells.

Recap

For any element, the Period number in the Periodic Table tells you the number of occupied electron shells in an atom of that element. The Group number (for Groups I to VII) tells you the number of outer shell electrons.

(d) (i) Bromine molecules have covalent bonding.
State what is meant by the term covalent bond. [2]

A covalent bond is a pair of electrons that is shared between two atoms.

Recap

A single covalent bond is formed when atoms share a pair of electrons so that the outer electrons of each atom achieve a stable, noble gas configuration (usually a full outer shell).

(d) (ii) Name one halide ion which bromine molecules can displace. [1]

Bromine can displace iodide ions (or astatide/tennesside ions).

Recap

A more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide salt. The reactivity of halogens decreases down the group (F > Cl > Br > I).

(d) (iii) Explain why bromine can displace the halide ion in (d)(ii). [1]

Bromine can displace iodide ions because bromine is more reactive than iodine.

Recap

The reactivity of the halogens decreases down the group. This is because atoms higher up the group can gain an electron more easily to complete their outer shell, as their outer shell is closer to the nucleus and less shielded.

(e) Name a halide compound which can be used to detect the presence of water. [2]

An anhydrous halide compound used to test for water is cobalt(II) chloride.

Recap

Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride paper is used as a chemical test for water. It turns from blue to pink when water is added. Another test is using anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, which turns from white to blue.

(f) Calcium chloride is an ionic compound.
Complete the dot-and-cross diagram in Fig. 2.1 for the ions in calcium chloride.
Give the charges on each of the ions.

(A diagram showing a central Ca ion with 8 crosses in its outer shell (shell 3) and a [2+] charge, flanked by two Cl ions, each with 7 dots and 1 cross in their outer shell (shell 3) and a [–] charge.)

The central Ca ion should show 8 crosses in its third shell and a charge of [2+].
Each Cl ion should show 7 dots and 1 cross in its third shell and a charge of [–].

Recap

An ionic bond is the electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions. Metals (like Calcium in Group II) lose electrons to form positive ions. Non-metals (like Chlorine in Group VII) gain electrons to form negative ions.

(g) (i) Aqueous lead(II) ions are added to aqueous chloride ions. A white precipitate of insoluble lead(II) chloride, PbCl₂, is formed.
Name a lead(II) compound which can be used in this reaction. [1]

A suitable soluble lead(II) compound is lead(II) nitrate.

Recap

To form an insoluble salt by precipitation, you must mix two soluble salts. According to solubility rules, all nitrates are soluble, making lead(II) nitrate a good choice to provide lead(II) ions in solution.

(g) (ii) Write the ionic equation for this reaction. Include state symbols. [3]

The ionic equation is: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) → PbCl₂(s).

Recap

Insoluble salts are made by mixing two soluble salts. This is a precipitation reaction. An ionic equation shows only the ions that react to form the precipitate (the solid product). Other ions, like the nitrate ions (NO₃⁻) and the cation with the chloride (e.g., Na⁺), are spectator ions and are omitted.

(g) (iii) Name one other insoluble chloride. [1]

One other insoluble chloride is silver chloride (AgCl).

Recap

The general solubility rules state that most chlorides are soluble, but halides of silver and lead are notable insoluble salts.

This question is about acids, bases and alkalis.

Table 3.1 shows the pH values of some substances.

(a) Define the term base. [1]

A base is a proton acceptor (a proton is an H⁺ ion).

Recap

Acids and bases can be defined in terms of proton transfer. An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor. A base is a proton (H⁺) acceptor. For example, in the reaction HCl + NH₃ → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻, the HCl donates a proton and the NH₃ accepts it.

(b) State what is meant by the term alkali. [1]

An alkali is a soluble base.

Recap

Bases are substances which react with acids to produce a salt and water (e.g., metal oxides and hydroxides). Alkalis are bases which are soluble in water and produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in solution.

(c) Thymolphthalein is an indicator. State the colour of thymolphthalein in:

  • NaOH(aq)
  • CH₃COOH(aq)
[2]

In NaOH(aq) (an alkali), thymolphthalein is blue.
In CH₃COOH(aq) (an acid), it is colourless.

Recap

Indicators change colour at different pH values. Thymolphthalein indicator is colourless in acid (and neutral solutions) and blue in alkali. Methyl orange is red in acid and yellow in alkali.

(d) (i) Use the information in Table 3.1 to identify the substance with the highest concentration of H⁺(aq) ions.
Explain your answer. [2]

Substance: HNO₃(aq)
Explanation: It has the lowest pH (pH 1). A lower pH value indicates a higher concentration of H⁺(aq) ions.

Recap

The pH scale is a measure of acidity or alkalinity. Acids have pH values below 7, and alkalis have pH values above 7. A strong acid has a very low pH (e.g., 0-2), while a weak acid has a pH closer to 7 (e.g., 3-6).

(d) (ii) Name an indicator which can be used to identify the substance with the highest concentration of H⁺(aq) ions. [1]

Universal indicator can be used, as it would show a specific colour (e.g., red) corresponding to pH 1 on a colour chart.

Recap

Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators which is used to determine the pH of a solution by comparing its colour to the colours on a pH colour chart. This allows you to find an approximate pH value, not just whether it is an acid or alkali.

(e) Complete the equation to show the dissociation of ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, in aqueous solution. [3]

CH₃COOH(aq) ...

CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq)

Recap

Ethanoic acid is a weak acid, which means it is only partially dissociated (or ionised) in aqueous solution. Therefore, the equilibrium sign (⇌) must be used. Strong acids, like HNO₃, are completely dissociated and use a standard arrow (→).

(f) Write the ionic equation which represents a neutralisation reaction between any acid and any alkali. [1]

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Recap

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid (which provides H⁺ ions) and an alkali (which provides OH⁻ ions) to form water. The salt ions (e.g., Na⁺ and Cl⁻) are spectator ions.

(g) Dilute nitric acid, HNO₃(aq), reacts with aqueous calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂(aq), as shown.
2HNO₃(aq) + Ca(OH)₂(aq) → Ca(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
20.0 cm³ of 0.0150 mol/dm³ Ca(OH)₂(aq) reacts with 25.0 cm³ of HNO₃(aq)
Calculate the concentration of HNO₃(aq) in g/dm³.
Use the following steps.

  • Calculate the number of moles of Ca(OH)₂(aq) used.
  • Determine the number of moles of HNO₃(aq) which react with the Ca(OH)₂(aq).
  • Calculate the concentration of HNO₃(aq) in mol/dm³.
  • Calculate the concentration of HNO₃(aq) in g/dm³.
[5]

Moles of Ca(OH)₂:
Volume = 20.0 cm³ = 0.0200 dm³
Moles = Concentration × Volume = 0.0150 mol/dm³ × 0.0200 dm³ = 0.000300 mol.

Moles of HNO₃:
From the balanced equation, the mole ratio HNO₃ : Ca(OH)₂ is 2:1.
Moles HNO₃ = 2 × Moles Ca(OH)₂ = 2 × 0.000300 mol = 0.000600 mol.

Concentration of HNO₃ (mol/dm³):
Volume = 25.0 cm³ = 0.0250 dm³
Concentration = Moles / Volume = 0.000600 mol / 0.0250 dm³ = 0.0240 mol/dm³.

Concentration of HNO₃ (g/dm³):
First, find the Mr of HNO₃: Mr = 1 (for H) + 14 (for N) + (3 × 16) (for O) = 63 g/mol.
Concentration (g/dm³) = Concentration (mol/dm³) × Mr = 0.0240 mol/dm³ × 63 g/mol = 1.512 g/dm³. (1.51 is also acceptable)

Recap

A titration calculation involves several steps:
1. Convert all volumes to dm³ (divide cm³ by 1000).
2. Calculate the moles of the substance with a known concentration and volume (moles = conc × vol).
3. Use the mole ratio from the balanced stoichiometric equation to find the moles of the unknown substance.
4. Calculate the concentration of the unknown substance (conc = moles / vol).
5. If needed, convert mol/dm³ to g/dm³ by multiplying by the Mr.

Topics: Organic Chemistry, Esters, Chemical reactions, Equilibrium

The equation for the reaction between methanoic acid and ethanol in the presence of a catalyst can be represented as shown.

HCOOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ X + H₂O     ΔH = –29.5 kJ / mol

X represents the ester formed.

(a) (i) In the equation, methanoic acid is represented by the formula HCOOH.
Name this type of formula. [1]

This type of formula (HCOOH), which shows how atoms are arranged, is a structural formula (specifically, a condensed structural formula).

Recap

A structural formula is an unambiguous (clearly defined) description of the way the atoms are arranged in a compound. For example, the structural formula of propan-1-ol is CH₃CH₂CH₂OH. The displayed formula shows all atoms and all bonds.

(a) (ii) Write the empirical formula of methanoic acid. [1]

The molecular formula of methanoic acid (HCOOH) is CH₂O₂. Since this is the simplest whole number ratio, the empirical formula is also CH₂O₂.

Recap

The empirical formula is the simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms in a compound. The molecular formula shows the number and type of each atom in a molecule.

(b) Name and draw the displayed formula of ester X. [3]

Name: ethyl methanoate

Displayed Formula:

(A diagram showing H-C(=O)-O-CH₂-CH₃, with all H atoms also shown)

Recap

Esters are formed when a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol in the presence of an acid catalyst. When naming esters, the name begins with the alkyl group from the alcohol (e.g., 'ethyl' from ethanol) and ends with the part from the carboxylic acid (e.g., 'methanoate' from methanoic acid).

(c) (i) The reaction is reversible and reaches an equilibrium within a closed system.
State what is meant by the term closed system. [1]

A closed system is one where nothing can enter or leave; there is no loss or gain of matter with the surroundings.

Recap

An equilibrium reaction can only take place in a closed system. If it were an open system (like an unsealed tube), a gaseous product could escape, preventing the reverse reaction from occurring at the same rate.

(c) (ii) State two characteristics of an equilibrium. [2]

1. The rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the backward (reverse) reaction.
2. The concentrations of all reactants and products remain constant (at a constant temperature and pressure).

Recap

An equilibrium reaction is dynamic. Reactants are continuously forming products, and products are continuously forming reactants. At equilibrium, these two processes occur at the same rate, so there is no overall change in the amounts of each substance.

(c) (iii) Complete Table 4.1 to show the effect, if any, on the concentration of X at equilibrium for each change of condition. [4]

change of condition effect on the concentration of X at equilibrium
temperature is decreased Increases
concentration of HCOOH is decreased Decreases
concentrations of both HCOOH and CH₃CH₂OH are decreased Decreases
the catalyst is removed No effect
  • Temperature is decreased: Increases. The forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH = –29.5). Decreasing the temperature favours the exothermic direction to release heat, so the equilibrium shifts right, producing more X.
  • Concentration of HCOOH is decreased: Decreases. The equilibrium shifts to the left to oppose the change (to try and replace the lost HCOOH), consuming X.
  • Concentrations of both reactants are decreased: Decreases. The equilibrium shifts to the left (the side with more moles of reactants, 2) to oppose the decrease in concentration/pressure.
  • The catalyst is removed: No effect. A catalyst does not affect the position of equilibrium; it only increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached.

Recap

Le Chatelier's Principle states that if a change is made to the conditions of a system at equilibrium, the system will move to oppose the change.
• Temperature: Increasing T favours the endothermic direction. Decreasing T favours the exothermic direction.
• Pressure: Increasing P favours the side with fewer moles of gas.
• Concentration: Increasing a reactant's concentration shifts equilibrium to the products. Removing a product shifts equilibrium to the products.
• Catalyst: No effect on equilibrium position, only on the rate.

Topics: Particle theory, Organic Chemistry, Alkanes, Alkenes, Polymers

Butane and but-1-ene are colourless gases at room temperature and pressure.

(a) Suggest why but-1-ene diffuses quicker than butane. [1]

But-1-ene (C₄H₈, Mr = 56) has a lower relative molecular mass than butane (C₄H₁₀, Mr = 58). Lighter particles move and diffuse quicker.

Recap

Diffusion is the spreading out and mixing of different particles because of their random movement. The rate of diffusion is faster the lower the relative molecular mass of a substance.

(b) Identify the products formed when butane undergoes complete combustion. [1]

The products of complete combustion are carbon dioxide and water.

Recap

Hydrocarbons are compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen. The complete combustion of hydrocarbons (in plentiful oxygen) produces carbon dioxide and water. Incomplete combustion (in limited oxygen) produces carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot).

(c) (i) One molecule of butane reacts with one molecule of chlorine in the presence of ultraviolet light.
Name the type of reaction which needs ultraviolet light. [1]

This is a photochemical reaction.

Recap

A photochemical reaction is a reaction which depends on the presence of light. The light provides the activation energy needed to break the bonds (e.g., the Cl-Cl bond) and initiate the reaction.

(c) (ii) State the purpose of ultraviolet light during this reaction. [1]

The purpose of the ultraviolet light is to provide the activation energy.

Recap

Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have in order to react. In this photochemical reaction, UV light supplies this energy.

(c) (iii) Name the type of reaction which takes place when one atom of chlorine replaces one atom of hydrogen. [1]

This is a substitution reaction.

Recap

Alkanes are saturated and generally unreactive. They undergo substitution reactions with halogens, where a hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen atom. A substitution reaction is one in which one atom or group of atoms replaces another.

(c) (iv) Determine how many different structural isomers can form during this reaction. [1]

2 structural isomers can form.
(1-chlorobutane, where Cl replaces a hydrogen on carbon 1 or 4; and 2-chlorobutane, where Cl replaces a hydrogen on carbon 2 or 3).

Recap

Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula (in this case, C₄H₉Cl) but different structural formulae (different arrangement of atoms). Butane has two different types of hydrogen atom that can be substituted.

(d) (i) When but-1-ene reacts with steam, two possible products form.
Identify the type of catalyst which is used in this reaction. [1]

An acid catalyst is used (e.g., concentrated phosphoric(V) acid adsorbed on silica).

Recap

Alkenes react with steam (an addition reaction) to form alcohols. The conditions needed are high temperature (e.g., 300 °C), high pressure (e.g., 6000 kPa or 60 atm), and an acid catalyst.
