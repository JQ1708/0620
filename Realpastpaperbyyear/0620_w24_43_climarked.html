(a) State the meaning of the term electrolysis. [2]

Electrolysis is the breakdown (or decomposition) of an ionic compound, when it is either molten (liquid) or in an aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

Recap

The substance being broken down is called the electrolyte. The positive electrode is the anode and the negative electrode is the cathode. During electrolysis, positive ions (cations) move to the cathode and negative ions (anions) move to the anode.

At the anode (positive electrode), negative ions lose electrons (this is oxidation). In the electrolysis of aqueous copper(II) sulfate (using inert electrodes), hydroxide ions (OH⁻) from the water are discharged in preference to sulfate ions, producing oxygen gas and water.

(c) Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed using copper electrodes instead of graphite electrodes.

The copper anode is not inert; it reacts and undergoes oxidation. Copper atoms from the anode lose electrons to become copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) which then dissolve into the solution.

Recap

This process is used in the purification of copper. The impure copper is used as the anode, and it dissolves. Pure copper is then deposited onto the cathode. The ionic half-equation at the anode is: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻.

The product at the cathode is copper.

Recap

At the cathode (negative electrode), positive ions gain electrons (this is reduction). Copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) from the electrolyte are attracted to the cathode and are deposited as pure copper metal. The ionic half-equation is: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s).

There is no change in the appearance (the blue colour) of the solution.

Recap

The blue colour of the solution is due to copper(II) ions. For every Cu²⁺ ion that is removed from the solution at the cathode, one Cu²⁺ ion is added to the solution from the copper anode. Therefore, the concentration of copper(II) ions remains constant.

(b) SnO₂ is an amphoteric oxide. SnO₂ reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide, NaOH, to form a sodium salt and water only. The sodium salt contains a negative ion with the formula SnO₃²⁻.

(i) State the meaning of the term amphoteric. [1]

An amphoteric substance (usually an oxide or hydroxide) is one that can react with both acids and bases to form a salt and water.

Recap

Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) and zinc oxide (ZnO) are common examples of amphoteric oxides. They act as bases when reacting with acids and as acidic oxides when reacting with strong alkalis.

(ii) Write the symbol equation for the reaction between SnO₂ and NaOH. [2]

SnO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SnO₃ + H₂O

Recap

As an amphoteric oxide, tin(IV) oxide acts as an acidic oxide when it reacts with a strong base like sodium hydroxide. This neutralisation reaction produces a salt (sodium stannate, Na₂SnO₃) and water. The equation must be balanced.

(c) Tin is a metal that forms both covalent and ionic compounds. Suggest why this is unusual for a metal. [1]

This is unusual because metals typically lose electrons to form positive ions, resulting in ionic bonds with non-metals. They do not usually share electrons to form covalent bonds.

Recap

The type of bond formed (ionic or covalent) depends on the difference in electronegativity. Metals have low electronegativity and non-metals have high electronegativity, which leads to ionic bonding (electron transfer). Covalent bonding (electron sharing) usually occurs between two non-metals.

(d) (i) Tin(IV) chloride, SnCl₄, is covalently bonded. A tin atom has four electrons in its outer shell. Complete the dot-and-cross diagram in Fig. 3.1 for a molecule of SnCl₄. Show the outer shell electrons only. [2]

The completed diagram should show:

  1. A single dot-and-cross pair of electrons shared between the central Sn atom and each of the four Cl atoms.
  2. Three pairs of non-bonding electrons (lone pairs) on each of the four Cl atoms. The Sn atom should have no lone pairs.

Recap

Tin is in Group IV, so it has 4 outer shell electrons. Chlorine is in Group VII, so it has 7 outer shell electrons. The tin atom forms a single covalent bond with each of the four chlorine atoms, sharing a total of 4 pairs of electrons. This gives the tin atom a stable outer shell of 8 electrons. Each chlorine atom shares one pair and has 3 lone pairs, also giving it a stable outer shell of 8 electrons.

(ii) Tin(II) oxide, SnO, is ionically bonded. The melting points of SnCl₄ and SnO are shown in Table 3.1. Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why SnCl₄ has a much lower melting point than SnO. [3]

SnCl₄ is a simple molecular substance. It has weak intermolecular forces of attraction between its molecules, which require very little energy to overcome.

SnO is a giant ionic lattice. It has strong electrostatic forces of attraction (ionic bonds) between positive Sn²⁺ ions and negative O²⁻ ions. A large amount of energy is needed to break these strong bonds, resulting in a high melting point.

Recap

The melting point of a substance depends on the strength of the forces *between* its particles. Simple molecules (like SnCl₄) have weak intermolecular forces. Giant structures (like SnO) have strong ionic bonds that must be broken.

(e) Part of the reactivity series is shown. [Mg, Al, Sn, Cu]

(i) ...Explain why a reaction [Al + tin(II) sulfate] does not occur. [1]

Aluminium is covered by a very strong, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), which forms instantly in air. This protective layer prevents the underlying aluminium metal from reacting with the tin(II) sulfate solution.

Recap

Although aluminium is high in the reactivity series, its oxide layer makes it resistant to corrosion and reaction. If this layer is removed (e.g., by scratching or reacting with an alkali), the aluminium will react vigorously.

(ii) ...Write an ionic equation for any reaction that occurs... [2]

Experiment 1 (Copper is added to aqueous tin(II) sulfate): no reaction

Experiment 2 (Magnesium is added to aqueous tin(II) sulfate): Mg(s) + Sn²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Sn(s)

Recap

A displacement reaction occurs if the solid metal is more reactive than the metal in the aqueous solution. Magnesium is more reactive than tin, so it displaces tin (Mg is oxidised, Sn²⁺ is reduced). Copper is less reactive than tin, so no reaction occurs.

(f) Hydrated tin(II) nitrate, Sn(NO₃)₂·20H₂O, decomposes when it is heated.

(i) State what is meant by the term hydrated. [1]

A hydrated substance is a solid compound that contains water of crystallisation chemically combined within its crystal structure.

Recap

A substance that does *not* contain water of crystallisation is called anhydrous. The water is shown in the formula after a dot, e.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O.

(ii) Complete the equation for the decomposition of Sn(NO₃)₂·20H₂O. [2]

2Sn(NO₃)₂·20H₂O → ...SnO + ...NO₂ + O₂ + ...H₂O

2Sn(NO₃)₂·20H₂O → 2SnO + 4NO₂ + O₂ + 40H₂O

Recap

Balancing equations involves ensuring that there is the same number of each type of atom on both the reactant and product side of the equation.
Reactants (left): Sn=2, N=4, O = (2x3x2) + (20x2x1) = 12 + 40 = 52, H = 20x2x2 = 80.
Products (right): Sn=2, N=4, O = (2x1) + (4x2) + (1x2) + (40x1) = 2 + 8 + 2 + 40 = 52, H = 40x2 = 80.
The equation is balanced.

(b) 25.0 cm³ of aqueous sodium hydroxide, NaOH, of concentration 0.100 mol/dm³ is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄... Calculate the concentration of H₂SO₄... [3]

Step 1: Calculate the number of moles of NaOH used.
Moles = concentration x volume (in dm³)
Moles of NaOH = 0.100 mol/dm³ x (25.0 / 1000) dm³
Moles of NaOH = 0.00250 mol

Step 2: Determine the number of moles of H₂SO₄ that react.
From the equation: 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
The mole ratio is 2 (NaOH) : 1 (H₂SO₄).
Moles of H₂SO₄ = Moles of NaOH / 2
Moles of H₂SO₄ = 0.00250 / 2 = 0.00125 mol

Step 3: Calculate the concentration of H₂SO₄.
Concentration = moles / volume (in dm³)
Concentration of H₂SO₄ = 0.00125 mol / (20.0 / 1000) dm³
Concentration of H₂SO₄ = 0.0625 mol/dm³

Recap

Titration calculations follow key steps:

  1. Calculate moles of the known solution (concentration x volume).
  2. Use the molar ratio from the balanced equation to find moles of the unknown solution.
  3. Calculate concentration of the unknown solution (moles / volume).

(c) A student is provided with an aqueous solution of sodium sulfate. Describe how to prepare a pure sample of sodium sulfate crystals from this solution. [3]

1. Heat the solution in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water. 2. Continue heating until the solution becomes saturated (at the point of crystallisation). This can be tested by dipping a glass rod in the solution and seeing if crystals form on it as it cools. 3. Allow the saturated solution to cool slowly, which causes crystals of sodium sulfate to form. 4. Filter the crystals from the remaining solution and dry them, for example, by pressing them between pieces of filter paper.

Recap

To obtain hydrated crystals (like sodium sulfate) from a solution, the solution must not be evaporated to dryness. It should be heated to the crystallisation point and then cooled to allow crystals to form. Evaporating to dryness would produce an anhydrous powder.

(d) Potassium hydrogen sulfate, KHSO₄, can be prepared by a reaction between aqueous potassium hydroxide and dilute sulfuric acid. Water is the only other product. Write a symbol equation for this reaction. [1]

(f) 0.325g of Zn is added to dilute sulfuric acid which contains 0.0100 moles of H₂SO₄.

(i) Determine whether Zn or H₂SO₄ is the limiting reactant. Explain your answer. [2]

Step 1: Calculate moles of Zn.
Moles = mass / Ar
Moles of Zn = 0.325 g / 65 = 0.005 mol.

Step 2: Compare moles using the 1:1 ratio.
Moles of H₂SO₄ available = 0.0100 mol.
From the equation Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂, the reaction ratio is 1:1.
Therefore, 0.005 mol of Zn only needs 0.005 mol of H₂SO₄ to react.

Conclusion:
Since we have 0.0100 mol of H₂SO₄ (which is more than the 0.005 mol needed), the H₂SO₄ is in excess.
The Zn is the limiting reactant because it will be completely used up first.

Recap

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is not in excess and is completely used up first, which stops the reaction. To find it, you must calculate the moles of each reactant and compare them using the molar ratio from the balanced equation.

(ii) ...Calculate the number of molecules in 48.0 cm³ of H₂ gas measured at r.t.p. [2]

Step 1: Convert volume to dm³.
Volume in dm³ = 48.0 cm³ / 1000 = 0.0480 dm³.

Step 2: Calculate moles of H₂ gas.
Moles = Volume / Molar Gas Volume (at r.t.p.)
Moles of H₂ = 0.0480 dm³ / 24 dm³/mol = 0.00200 mol.

Step 3: Calculate the number of molecules.
Number of molecules = moles x Avogadro constant
Number of molecules = 0.00200 x (6.02 x 10²³)
= 1.204 x 10²¹ molecules.

Recap

There are two key constants to remember for these calculations:

  1. The Avogadro constant (6.02 x 10²³) is the number of particles (atoms, molecules, ions) in one mole of a substance.
  2. The molar gas volume at r.t.p. (room temperature and pressure) is 24 dm³ per mole for any gas.

(b) (i) Explain, in terms of collision theory, why the rate of reaction is higher at a higher temperature. [3]

At a higher temperature, the particles gain more kinetic energy and move faster. This leads to two effects:

  1. The particles collide more frequently.
  2. A greater proportion of these collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy.
This results in more successful collisions per unit of time, and therefore a faster rate of reaction.

Recap

Collision theory states that for a reaction to occur, particles must collide with the correct orientation and with sufficient energy. This minimum energy required is known as the activation energy (Eₐ).

  2. The line being steeper than the original line, showing a faster initial rate.
  3. The line levelling off at the same final mass, but at an earlier time.

Recap

A higher temperature means a faster rate, so the curve will be steeper and the reaction will finish faster (levels off earlier). Since all other conditions (like the amount of reactant) are the same, the total mass loss (and thus the final mass) will be the same.

(d) The reaction between nitrogen monoxide and oxygen can reach equilibrium. 2NO(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g)   ΔH = –113 kJ/mol

(i) Explain why the method shown in Fig. 5.3 will not allow the reaction to reach equilibrium. [1]

This is not a closed system. The gases (both reactants and products) can escape from the open beaker, so the reverse reaction (2NO₂ → 2NO + O₂) cannot happen at a rate that will eventually equal the forward reaction.

Recap

A closed system is one where no matter can enter or leave. For a reversible reaction involving gases to reach dynamic equilibrium (where the rates of the forward and reverse reactions are equal), it must be in a sealed container.

(ii) ...Explain why the position of equilibrium shifts to the left [when temperature is increased]. [1]

The forward reaction is exothermic, as shown by the negative ΔH value (–113 kJ/mol). When the temperature is increased, the system opposes this change by favouring the endothermic (reverse) reaction, thus shifting the position of equilibrium to the left (towards the reactants).

Recap

This is an example of Le Chatelier's Principle.

  • If temperature is increased, the equilibrium shifts in the endothermic (ΔH positive) direction to absorb the added heat.
  • If temperature is decreased, the equilibrium shifts in the exothermic (ΔH negative) direction to release heat.

(iii) The pressure ... is then increased. State the direction ... and explain your answer. [2]

Direction: The position of equilibrium shifts to the right.

Explanation: There are 3 moles of gas on the left side (2 NO + 1 O₂) and only 2 moles of gas on the right side (2 NO₂). When the pressure is increased, the system opposes the change by shifting to the side with fewer moles of gas to reduce the overall pressure.

Recap

According to Le Chatelier's Principle for pressure (in gaseous equilibria):

  • Increase pressure: Equilibrium shifts to the side with fewer moles of gas.
  • Decrease pressure: Equilibrium shifts to the side with more moles of gas.
  • If moles of gas are equal on both sides, pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium.
