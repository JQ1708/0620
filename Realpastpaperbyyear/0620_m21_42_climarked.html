(c) When separate samples of each of these gases are placed in a container they will diffuse. (i) Describe why these gases diffuse. [1]

These gases diffuse due to the random motion of molecules (or particles) in the gaseous state, causing them to spread out and mix.

Recap

▼

Diffusion is the spreading out and mixing of different particles because of their random movement. According to the kinetic particle theory, gas particles are in constant, rapid, random motion and bounce off each other and the container walls.

(ii) State which of these four gases has the highest rate of diffusion. Explain your answer. [2]

The gas with the highest rate of diffusion is hydrogen.

This is because hydrogen has the lowest relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) (Mᵣ(H₂)=2, Mᵣ(N₂)=28, Mᵣ(O₂)=32, Mᵣ(Cl₂)=71), and the rate of diffusion is faster for molecules with a lower Mᵣ.

Recap

▼

The rate of diffusion is inversely proportional to the square root of the mass of the particles (Graham's Law). Essentially, lighter particles move faster than heavier particles at the same temperature, thus diffusing more quickly.

Question 3 | Paper Code: 0620/42/F/M/21 Q3 | Topic: Chemical Equilibrium, Rates of Reaction, Calculations
This question is about ammonia. (a) Nitrogen reacts with hydrogen to form ammonia in an industrial process. N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ↔ 2NH₃(g)
(i) Name this industrial process. [1]

This industrial process is called the Haber process (or Haber-Bosch process).

Recap

▼

The Haber process synthesizes ammonia from nitrogen (from air) and hydrogen (from natural gas/methane and steam) and is crucial for producing fertilisers.

(ii) State the meaning of the symbol ↔. [1]

The symbol ↔ means the reaction is reversible or has reached a state of equilibrium.

Recap

▼

A reversible reaction is one where the products can react to re-form the original reactants. At equilibrium, the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.

(iii) State the conditions used in this industrial process. Include units. [2]

Temperature: 450 °C

Pressure: 200 atm (or 20 000 kPa)

Recap

▼

The temperature and pressure chosen for the Haber process are a compromise. The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature gives a higher yield, but 450 °C is used for a faster rate of reaction.

(iv) Name the catalyst used in this industrial process. [1]

The catalyst used is iron (Fe).

Recap

▼

A catalyst increases the rate of reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy (Eₐ). Iron is cheap and effective for this industrial process and does not affect the position of equilibrium.

(v) If the pressure is increased, the yield of ammonia increases. Explain why, in terms of equilibrium. [2]

The equilibrium shifts to the forwards direction (to the right) because the right-hand side has a fewer number of molecules (or moles) of gas (2 moles on the right versus 4 moles on the left). This shift acts to oppose the increase in pressure, thus favouring the formation of more ammonia.

Recap

▼

The application of Le Châtelier's Principle states that increasing the pressure on a gaseous equilibrium mixture causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the fewer moles of gas to relieve the stress.

(vi) If the temperature is increased, the rate of reaction increases. Explain why, in terms of particles. [3]

When the temperature is increased, the particles have more energy (more kinetic energy). This means more collisions occur per second, and more importantly, a greater proportion of collisions are successful (they have energy above the activation energy) leading to a faster rate.

Recap

▼

The collision theory explains that an increase in temperature increases the kinetic energy of reacting particles. This results in both a higher frequency of collisions and a larger fraction of particles overcoming the activation energy (Eₐ).

(b) Ammonia reacts with sulfuric acid to make a compound which is used as a fertiliser. Write the chemical equation for the reaction between ammonia and sulfuric acid. [2]

The equation is: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

The compound formed is ammonium sulfate, which is used as an NPK fertiliser.

Recap

▼

Ammonia (NH₃) acts as a weak base (alkali) and reacts with the acid, sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), in a neutralisation reaction to form the salt (ammonium sulfate). Note that no water is produced when ammonia reacts with an acid.

Question 4 | Paper Code: 0620/42/F/M/21 Q4 | Topic: Salt Preparation, Solubility, Calculations
A student wanted to make some zinc chloride crystals. The student followed the procedure shown.

step 1 Add excess zinc powder to dilute hydrochloric acid to form aqueous zinc chloride.

step 2 Remove unreacted zinc powder from the aqueous zinc chloride.

step 3 Heat the solution until it is saturated.

step 4 Allow the saturated solution to cool and remove the crystals that form.

(a) Write the equation for the reaction in step 1. Include state symbols. [3]

The equation is: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Recap

▼

A reactive metal (Zinc is above Hydrogen in the reactivity series) reacts with an acid (hydrochloric acid) to produce a salt (zinc chloride) and hydrogen gas. The state symbols are (s) for solid, (aq) for aqueous solution, and (g) for gas.

(b) Explain why excess zinc powder is added in step 1. [1]

Excess zinc powder is added to make sure all the hydrochloric acid reacts (i.e., the acid is the limiting reactant), ensuring the final solution only contains the desired salt and excess solid, not excess acid.

Recap

▼

In preparing a soluble salt from an insoluble reactant (metal, metal oxide, or metal carbonate), the insoluble reactant must be added in excess. This ensures the resulting salt solution is pure (free of the original acid) and only needs to be filtered before crystallisation.

(c) Suggest how unreacted zinc powder is removed in step 2. [1]

Unreacted zinc powder is removed in step 2 by filtration.

Recap

▼

Filtration is used to separate a solid (the residue, in this case, unreacted zinc) from a liquid (the filtrate, which is the zinc chloride solution). Zinc is insoluble in the resulting solution, unlike the zinc chloride salt.

(d) A saturated solution is formed in step 3. Suggest what is meant by the term saturated solution. [2]

A saturated solution is one that can dissolve no more solute (zinc chloride) at a given temperature.

Recap

▼

The maximum amount of solute that can dissolve depends heavily on temperature. To get a saturated solution, the water content is often reduced by heating until the crystallisation point is reached.

(e) Explain why crystals form as the solution cools in step 4. [1]

Crystals form as the solution cools because the solubility (of zinc chloride or solids generally) decreases as the temperature decreases, meaning the excess solute precipitates out.

Recap

▼

For most salts, solubility increases with temperature. When a hot, saturated solution cools, the solution becomes supersaturated, forcing the dissolved solute molecules to bond together to form solid crystals (crystallisation).

(f) Name two zinc compounds which react with dilute hydrochloric acid to form zinc chloride. [2]

Two zinc compounds that react with dilute hydrochloric acid to form zinc chloride are zinc oxide (a base) and zinc carbonate (a carbonate).

Recap

▼

Acids react with: 1) Metals (salt + hydrogen), 2) Metal Oxides/Hydroxides (salt + water), and 3) Metal Carbonates (salt + water + carbon dioxide). Any combination of two from categories 1, 2, and 3 is correct, given the acid is hydrochloric acid.

(g) If excess calcium metal is used instead of excess zinc powder in step 1, pure calcium chloride crystals do not form. Explain why. [1]

If excess calcium metal is used, pure crystals do not form because calcium will also react with water, making the purification step overly complicated and yielding side products like calcium hydroxide.

Recap

▼

Calcium is high up in the reactivity series (Group II metal, more reactive than Zinc) and reacts with cold water to form a soluble metal hydroxide (calcium hydroxide) and hydrogen. This means the excess reactant would not be simple to filter off and would react with the water in the solution.

(h) Some salts can be made by titration. In a titration experiment, 20.0 cm³ of aqueous sodium hydroxide reacts exactly with 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ dilute sulfuric acid to make sodium sulfate. 2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) (i) Circle the name of the type of reaction that takes place. [1]

The type of reaction is neutralisation.

Recap

▼

A neutralisation reaction occurs between an acid (H₂SO₄) and a base (NaOH, which is a soluble base, an alkali) to form a salt and water. The net ionic equation is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

(ii) Calculate the concentration of the aqueous sodium hydroxide in g/dm³ using the following steps. [5]

Step 1: Calculate the number of moles of dilute sulfuric acid used.

Moles H₂SO₄ = Concentration x Volume (in dm³) Volume = 25.0 cm³ / 1000 = 0.0250 dm³ Moles H₂SO₄ = 0.100 mol/dm³ x 0.0250 dm³ = 0.00250 mol

Step 2: Determine the number of moles of sodium hydroxide which react with the dilute sulfuric acid.

From the equation (2NaOH : 1H₂SO₄ ratio): Moles NaOH = 2 x Moles H₂SO₄ Moles NaOH = 2 x 0.00250 mol = 0.00500 mol

Step 3: Calculate the concentration of the aqueous sodium hydroxide in mol/dm³.

Concentration NaOH = Moles / Volume (in dm³) Volume NaOH = 20.0 cm³ / 1000 = 0.0200 dm³ Concentration NaOH = 0.00500 mol / 0.0200 dm³ = 0.250 mol/dm³

Step 4: Calculate the concentration of the aqueous sodium hydroxide in g/dm³.

Mᵣ of NaOH (Na=23, O=16, H=1) = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 Concentration (g/dm³) = Concentration (mol/dm³) x Mᵣ Concentration (g/dm³) = 0.250 mol/dm³ x 40 g/mol = 10.0 g/dm³

Recap

▼

This titration calculation follows four steps: 1) Find moles of known reactant, 2) Use the mole ratio (stoichiometry) from the balanced equation to find moles of unknown reactant, 3) Find the concentration (mol/dm³) of the unknown reactant, and 4) Convert this concentration to g/dm³ using Mᵣ. Remember to always convert volumes to dm³ before calculation.
