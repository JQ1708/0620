Topics: Rates of Reaction, Calculations

Oxygen is produced by the decomposition of aqueous hydrogen peroxide. Manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂, is a catalyst for this reaction.

(a) State the meaning of the term catalyst. [2]

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction but remains chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.

Recap

A catalyst is a substance which increases the rate of a chemical reaction but is not used up in the reaction. Catalysts work by lowering the activation energy (Eₐ) of the reaction, which means a greater proportion of collisions will be successful.

(b) A student adds powdered manganese(IV) oxide to aqueous hydrogen peroxide in a conical flask as shown in Fig. 4.1. The mass of the conical flask and its contents is measured at regular time intervals. The mass decreases as time increases.

(i) State why the mass of the conical flask and its contents decreases as time increases. [1]

The mass decreases because oxygen, which is a gas, is produced during the reaction and escapes from the flask (through the cotton wool plug).

Recap

The progress of a reaction that produces a gas can be followed by measuring the loss of mass of the reaction mixture over time using a balance. This method works because the gas escapes from the reaction vessel, leading to a decrease in the total mass recorded.

(ii) Explain why the rate of reaction is highest at the start of the reaction. [1]

The rate is highest at the start because the concentration of the reactant (hydrogen peroxide) is at its highest. This leads to the highest frequency of collisions between reactant particles.

Recap

The rate of reaction is fastest at the beginning and decreases as the reaction proceeds. This is because the concentration of the reactants decreases, leading to fewer particles per unit volume and therefore a lower frequency of collisions.

(iii) Explain why the rate of reaction eventually becomes zero. [1]

The reaction stops (rate becomes zero) when one of the reactants, in this case the hydrogen peroxide, is completely used up. It is the limiting reactant.

Recap

A reaction stops when the limiting reactant is used up. The limiting reactant is the reactant that is not in excess and determines the total amount of product formed.

(c) The experiment is repeated at an increased temperature. All other conditions stay the same. Explain in terms of collision theory why the rate of reaction is higher at an increased temperature. [3]

Increasing the temperature increases the kinetic energy of the particles. This leads to two effects:

  1. The particles move faster, resulting in an increased frequency of collisions.
  2. A greater proportion of these collisions will have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, leading to more successful collisions.

Recap

Increasing the temperature increases the rate of reaction. According to collision theory, this is because particles gain more kinetic energy, causing them to move faster and collide more frequently. More importantly, a greater proportion of these colliding particles will have energy above the activation energy, leading to more effective (successful) collisions.

(d) The equation for the decomposition of aqueous hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂(aq), is shown. 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) $50.0~cm^{3}$ of a $0.200~mol/dm^{3}$ solution of H₂O₂(aq) is used. Calculate the mass of O₂ that forms. [3]

Step 1: Calculate the number of moles of H₂O₂ used.

Volume = $50.0~cm^{3} = 50.0 / 1000 = 0.0500~dm^{3}$

Moles = Concentration × Volume (in dm³)

Moles of H₂O₂ = $0.200~mol/dm^{3} \times 0.0500~dm^{3} = 0.0100~mol$


Step 2: Determine the number of moles of O₂ produced.

From the equation (2H₂O₂ → O₂), the stoichiometric ratio is 2 : 1.

Moles of O₂ = Moles of H₂O₂ / 2

Moles of O₂ = $0.0100~mol / 2 = 0.00500~mol$


Step 3: Calculate the mass of O₂ produced.

Relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) of O₂ = 16 + 16 = 32

Mass = Moles × Mᵣ

Mass of O₂ = $0.00500~mol \times 32~g/mol = 0.160~g$

Recap

To calculate reacting masses (stoichiometry):

  1. Calculate the moles of the substance with known mass or concentration (using moles = mass / Mᵣ or moles = concentration × volume).
  2. Use the stoichiometry (the molar ratio from the balanced chemical equation) to find the moles of the desired substance.
  3. Calculate the mass of the desired substance (using mass = moles × Mᵣ).

(e) State the effect on the mass of oxygen produced if the mass of powdered manganese(IV) oxide catalyst is increased. [1]

No effect.

A catalyst increases the rate of reaction but does not change the final amount (mass or moles) of product formed. The amount of product is determined by the limiting reactant (H₂O₂).

Recap

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction but is not used up in the reaction and does not alter the final yield of the products. The limiting reactant is the only thing that determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed.

(f) Oxygen can also be produced by the decomposition of mercury(II) oxide, HgO. The only products of this decomposition are mercury and oxygen. Write a symbol equation for this decomposition. [2]

2HgO → 2Hg + O₂

The initial equation is HgO → Hg + O₂. To balance the oxygen atoms, a 2 is placed before HgO (2HgO → Hg + O₂). This unbalances the mercury, so a 2 is placed before Hg to give the final balanced equation.

Recap

In a symbol equation, there must be the same number of each type of atom on both sides of the equation. Symbol equations are balanced by writing large numbers (coefficients) in front of the chemical formulae. You must never change the small numbers within a formula (e.g., changing H₂O to H₂O₂).

Electrolysis is the decomposition (breakdown) of an ionic compound, when molten or in an aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

Recap

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, known as the electrolyte, when it is molten or in aqueous solution by the passage of an electric current. Decomposition is the breakdown of a substance into two or more different substances.

(ii) State why graphite is suitable for use as an electrode. [1]

Graphite is suitable because it conducts electricity and is inert (it does not react with the electrolyte or the products).

Recap

Electrodes are typically made of graphite or platinum because they are good electrical conductors but are inert, meaning they do not react with the electrolyte. Graphite is a non-metal that conducts electricity due to delocalised electrons.

(iii) Write an ionic half-equation for the formation of hydrogen at the cathode. [2]

2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

At the cathode (negative electrode), positive ions (H⁺) gain electrons. This process is reduction.

Recap

Positive ions (cations) move to the cathode (negative electrode) where they gain electrons. This is reduction. Ionic half-equations are used to show this gain of electrons. For example, the formation of hydrogen from hydrogen ions is: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g).

(iv) Name the type of particle responsible for the transfer of charge in the conducting wires. [1]

Electrons

Recap

Charge transfer in the external circuit (the wires) involves the movement of electrons. These delocalised electrons flow from the anode towards the positive pole of the power supply, and from the negative pole to the cathode.

(v) Name the type of particle responsible for the transfer of charge in aqueous potassium bromide. [1]

Ions (specifically K⁺, Br⁻, H⁺, and OH⁻ ions)

Recap

In the electrolyte (the molten or aqueous solution), charge is transferred by the movement of ions. Positive ions (cations) move to the cathode, and negative ions (anions) move to the anode.

(vi) State the names of the products formed when electricity is passed through dilute aqueous potassium bromide using graphite electrodes. [2]

at the anode: Oxygen

at the cathode: Hydrogen

During the electrolysis of dilute aqueous ionic solutions (other than halides), water is preferentially discharged. This produces hydrogen gas at the cathode (from H⁺ ions in water) and oxygen gas at the anode (from OH⁻ ions in water). Even in this halide solution, because it is very dilute, oxygen is produced at the anode instead of bromine.

This question is about sulfur and compounds of sulfur. Sulfur is converted into sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, by the Contact process. The process involves four stages.

stage 1 Molten sulfur is converted into sulfur dioxide.
stage 2 Sulfur dioxide reacts with oxygen to form sulfur trioxide.
stage 3 Sulfur trioxide combines with concentrated sulfuric acid to form oleum, H₂S₂O₇.
stage 4 Oleum reacts to form concentrated sulfuric acid.

(a) (i) In stage 1, iron pyrites, FeS₂, can be used instead of molten sulfur. The iron pyrites is heated strongly in air. Balance the equation for the reaction occurring when iron pyrites reacts with oxygen in the air. [1]

.....FeS₂ + .....O₂ → .....Fe₂O₃ + .....SO₂

4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂

Recap

Balancing symbol equations ensures that the number of atoms of each element is the same on both the reactant and product sides. This equation is complex and balanced by inspecting and adjusting the coefficients (the large numbers in front) until all atoms are conserved.

(ii) Name Fe₂O₃. Include the oxidation number of iron. [1]

Iron(III) oxide

Oxygen almost always has an oxidation number of -2. Since there are three oxygen atoms, the total negative charge is -6. To balance this, the two iron atoms must have a total positive charge of +6. Therefore, each iron atom has an oxidation number of +3, which is shown by the Roman numeral (III).

Recap

Oxidation numbers give information about the degree of oxidation. Roman numerals are used to show the oxidation number of a particular element (usually a transition metal) in a compound. For example, in iron(II) oxide (FeO) the iron is +2, while in iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃) the iron is +3.

(b) The equation for stage 2 is shown. 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) The forward reaction is exothermic. The reaction is carried out at a temperature of 450 °C and a pressure of 2 atm.

Using explanations that do not involve cost:

(i) explain why a temperature greater than 450 °C is not used [1]

A temperature greater than 450 °C is not used because the forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH is negative). According to Le Chatelier's principle, increasing the temperature would shift the position of equilibrium to the left (favouring the reactants) to absorb the added heat. This would decrease the yield of sulfur trioxide.

Recap

For an exothermic reaction (ΔH is negative), an increase in temperature moves the position of equilibrium in the direction of the reactants (the endothermic direction), which decreases the yield of the product. A compromise temperature is used to get a reasonable rate of reaction and a good yield.

(ii) explain why a pressure lower than 2 atm is not used. [1]

A lower pressure would decrease the rate of reaction because the gas particles would be further apart, leading to a lower frequency of collisions. (Alternatively, a lower pressure would also shift the position of equilibrium to the left, towards the side with more moles of gas (3 moles vs 2 moles), which would decrease the yield of SO₃).

Recap

Changing pressure affects reactions involving gases. Increasing the pressure moves the position of equilibrium in the direction of the lower number of moles of gas molecules in the equation. It also increases the rate of reaction by increasing collision frequency. In the Contact process, a high yield is achieved even at low pressures, so a high pressure is not required.

(c) When sulfuric acid reacts with ammonia the salt produced is ammonium sulfate. Write the symbol equation for this reaction. [2]

2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) is a dibasic acid, providing two H⁺ ions. Ammonia (NH₃) is a base that accepts one H⁺ ion to become NH₄⁺. Therefore, two NH₃ molecules are needed to react with one H₂SO₄ molecule to form the salt ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Recap

Bases react with acids to produce a salt and water (this is neutralisation). Ammonia is a special case; it is a base that reacts with acids to form an ammonium salt, but no water is produced in the reaction (e.g., 2NH₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄(aq)).

(d) Lead(II) sulfate is an insoluble salt. Lead(II) sulfate can be made from aqueous ammonium sulfate using a precipitation reaction.

(i) Name a solution that can be added to aqueous ammonium sulfate to produce a precipitate of lead(II) sulfate. [1]

Lead(II) nitrate

To make an insoluble salt by precipitation, you must mix two soluble salts. Lead(II) sulfate (PbSO₄) is insoluble. We are given aqueous ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄), which is soluble. We need a soluble source of lead(II) ions. According to the solubility rules, all nitrates are soluble, so lead(II) nitrate (Pb(NO₃)₂) is a suitable choice.

Recap

Insoluble salts are made by precipitation, which involves mixing two soluble salts. You must know the general solubility rules:

  • All nitrates are soluble.
  • All Group I element salts and ammonium salts are soluble.
  • Most sulfates are soluble, *except* for lead, barium, and calcium sulfates.

(ii) Write an ionic equation for this precipitation reaction. Include state symbols. [3]

Pb²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → PbSO₄(s)

The full equation is Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + (NH₄)₂SO₄(aq) → PbSO₄(s) + 2NH₄NO₃(aq). The spectator ions (NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻) are removed to give the ionic equation, which only shows the ions that react to form the precipitate (solid).

Recap

Ionic equations are simplified symbol equations that show only the ions that take part in the reaction. Spectator ions (ions that are present but do not change state or charge) are omitted from the final equation. State symbols are (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas, and (aq) for aqueous solution.

(iii) The precipitate of lead(II) sulfate forms in an aqueous solution. Describe how pure lead(II) sulfate can be obtained from the mixture. [3]

  1. Filter the mixture using filter paper and a funnel to separate the solid residue (lead(II) sulfate) from the filtrate (solution).
  2. Wash the residue on the filter paper with distilled water to remove any soluble impurities (like ammonium nitrate).
  3. Dry the solid, for example, by pressing it between filter papers or leaving it in a low-temperature oven.

Recap

To prepare an insoluble salt by precipitation:

  1. Mix two suitable soluble salts.
  2. Filter off the precipitate (which is the residue).
  3. Wash the precipitate with distilled water to remove soluble impurities.
  4. Dry the precipitate (e.g., between filter papers or in a warm oven).

(ii) There are three functional groups in compound A. Name the homologous series of compounds that contain the following functional groups: [3]

−C=C−

−OH

−COOH

−C=C− : Alkene

Recap

A homologous series is a family of similar compounds with similar chemical properties due to the presence of the same functional group.

  • The alkene series contains the C=C functional group.
  • The alcohol series contains the –OH (hydroxyl) functional group.
  • The carboxylic acid series contains the –COOH (carboxyl) functional group.

(iii) State what is observed when compound A is added to: [2]

aqueous bromine

aqueous sodium carbonate

aqueous bromine: The solution turns from orange (or yellow/brown) to colourless. This is because the −C=C− group makes the molecule unsaturated and it undergoes an addition reaction with bromine.

aqueous sodium carbonate: Bubbles of gas are produced (or fizzing / effervescence). This is because the −COOH group is acidic and reacts with carbonates to produce carbon dioxide gas.

Recap

Test for Unsaturation (Alkenes): Unsaturated hydrocarbons (containing C=C bonds) will turn aqueous bromine from orange to colourless in an addition reaction.

Test for Carboxylic Acids: Carboxylic acids are weak acids. They show typical acid reactions, such as reacting with carbonates (like sodium carbonate) to produce a salt, water, and carbon dioxide (which is seen as fizzing).

(iv) Compound A can be used as a single monomer to produce two different polymers. Draw one repeat unit of the addition polymer formed from compound A. [2]

The addition polymer forms by opening the C=C double bond. The other functional groups (–COOH and –CH₂OH) are side chains on the polymer backbone.

The repeat unit shows the two carbons from the double bond now as a single bond, with continuation bonds extending from them. The groups attached to these carbons (H, COOH, H, CH₂OH) are shown as side groups.

Recap

In addition polymerisation, the C=C double bond in the monomer breaks and changes to a C-C single bond, allowing the monomers to join together to form a long polymer chain. The repeat unit is the part of the polymer that is regularly repeated, and it has the same atoms as the monomer but a different bond structure.

(v) Compound A can be converted into a dicarboxylic acid. Name the type of condensation polymer formed from a dicarboxylic acid and a diol. [1]

Polyester

A dicarboxylic acid (a monomer with two –COOH groups) reacts with a diol (a monomer with two –OH groups) in a condensation reaction to form a polymer linked by ester linkages (–COO–).

Recap

Polyesters are condensation polymers. They are made by combining a dicarboxylic acid (HOOC-[]-COOH) with a diol (HO-[]-OH). Water is eliminated as a small molecule, and the monomers are joined by ester linkages (–COO–).
