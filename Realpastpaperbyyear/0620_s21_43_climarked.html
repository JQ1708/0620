Paper: 0620/43/M/J/21 Q1

Topics: Separation Techniques, Polymers, Organic Chemistry, Electrochemistry

Give the name of the process that is used:

(a) to produce large molecules from monomers [1]

The process is polymerisation.

Recap

Polymers are large molecules built up from many small, repeating units called monomers. The reaction to join them is called polymerisation. For example, in addition polymerisation, many alkene monomers join together by opening their C=C double bonds to form a long single-chain polymer, with no other product being formed.

(b) to separate oxygen from liquid air [1]

The process is fractional distillation.

Recap

Fractional distillation is a technique used to separate a mixture of liquids that have different boiling points. Liquid air is a mixture of liquids (mainly nitrogen and oxygen). As the liquid air is warmed, the substance with the lower boiling point (nitrogen, –196 °C) boils first, rises up the fractionating column, and is collected. Oxygen, with a higher boiling point (–183 °C), is collected lower down.

(c) to make ethanol from glucose [1]

The process is fermentation.

Recap

Fermentation is the process where glucose is broken down by enzymes found in yeast to produce ethanol and carbon dioxide. This process must be carried out in the absence of air (anaerobic conditions) and at an optimum temperature of around 25–35 °C.

(d) to separate water from aqueous sodium chloride [1]

The process is distillation (or simple distillation).

Recap

Simple distillation is used to separate a pure solvent (like water) from a solute (like sodium chloride) that is dissolved in it. The solution is heated, the water boils to form steam (vapour), which then passes into a condenser. The condenser cools the steam, turning it back into pure liquid water, which is then collected.

(e) to produce aluminium from aluminium oxide in molten cryolite [1]

The process is electrolysis.

Recap

Electrolysis is the decomposition (breakdown) of an ionic compound (the electrolyte) by passing an electric current through it when it is molten or in aqueous solution. Aluminium is a reactive metal, so it must be extracted from its ore (aluminium oxide) using electrolysis. Cryolite is added to lower the melting point and dissolve the aluminium oxide.

(f) to separate the products of hydrolysis of long chain carbohydrates [1]

The process is chromatography.

Recap

Paper chromatography is a technique used to separate mixtures of soluble substances (e.g., sugars, amino acids, or dyes). The hydrolysis of carbohydrates produces sugars, which are colourless. After running the chromatogram, a locating agent must be sprayed on the paper to react with the colourless spots and make them visible.

(g) to separate an aqueous solution from an undissolved solid. [1]

The process is filtration.

Recap

Filtration is a method used to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid or solution. The mixture is poured through filter paper; the liquid (the filtrate) passes through the pores, while the solid (the residue) is trapped by the paper.

Working:

  • 11B: The proton number is 5 (from table). In a neutral atom, protons = electrons, so it is 5. The mass number is 11. Neutrons = Mass Number – Protons = 11 – 5 = 6.
  • 35Cl–: The proton number is 17. It has a 1– charge, meaning it has gained one electron. Electrons = 17 + 1 = 18. The mass number is 35. Neutrons = 35 – 17 = 18.

Recap

The mass number (or nucleon number) is the total count of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Ions are formed when atoms lose electrons (forming positive ions, or cations) or gain electrons (forming negative ions, or anions).

Sodium reacts with fluorine to form sodium fluoride, NaF.

(a) Write a chemical equation for this reaction. [2]

2Na + F₂ → 2NaF

Recap

When writing chemical equations, you must ensure they are balanced, meaning there are equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides. Remember that halogens, like fluorine, exist as diatomic molecules (F₂). Sodium (a Group I metal) and fluorine (a Group VII halogen) react vigorously to form an ionic salt.

(b) Sodium fluoride is an ionic compound.

Complete the diagram to show the electron arrangement in the outer shells of the ions present in sodium fluoride.

Give the charges on both ions. [3]

The completed diagram would show:

  • The sodium ion, [Na]+, with a full outer shell of 8 electrons (originally shown as crosses). It has a charge of 1+.
  • The fluoride ion, [F]–, with a full outer shell of 8 electrons (7 original electrons, e.g., dots, and 1 gained electron, e.g., a cross from sodium). It has a charge of 1–.

Recap

An ionic bond forms from the electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions. This typically happens between a metal (like sodium) and a non-metal (like fluorine). The metal atom loses its outer shell electrons to form a positive ion (cation). The non-metal atom gains these electrons to form a negative ion (anion). Both ions achieve a stable noble gas electronic configuration.

(c) Aqueous sodium fluoride undergoes electrolysis.

(i) State what is meant by the term electrolysis. [2]

Electrolysis is the breakdown (or decomposition) of an ionic compound, when in the molten state or in aqueous solution, by the passage of electricity (or electric current).

Recap

The key components of electrolysis are:
1. An electrolyte: the ionic compound (molten or aqueous) that conducts electricity and is decomposed.
2. An electric current: provides the energy for the chemical change.
3. Decomposition: the electrolyte is broken down into simpler substances.

(ii) Name the products formed at the positive electrode (anode) and the negative electrode (cathode) when dilute aqueous sodium fluoride undergoes electrolysis. [2]

anode ..............................................

cathode ..............................................

anode: oxygen

cathode: hydrogen

Recap

In the electrolysis of a dilute aqueous solution, the ions from the water (H⁺ and OH⁻) are often discharged.
At the cathode (negative): Sodium (Na⁺) is a very reactive metal, much more reactive than hydrogen (H⁺). Therefore, H⁺ ions are preferentially discharged, gaining electrons to form hydrogen gas (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂).
At the anode (positive): The F⁻ ion is present, but in a dilute solution, OH⁻ ions (from water) are more easily discharged. OH⁻ ions lose electrons to form oxygen gas and water (4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻).

(d) Molten sodium fluoride undergoes electrolysis.

(i) Name the products formed at the positive electrode (anode) and the negative electrode (cathode) when molten sodium fluoride undergoes electrolysis. [2]

anode ..............................................

cathode ..............................................

anode: fluorine

cathode: sodium

Recap

When an ionic compound is molten, there is no water present. The only ions available are Na⁺ and F⁻.
At the cathode (negative): The positive Na⁺ ions are attracted, gain electrons (are reduced), and form liquid sodium metal.
At the anode (positive): The negative F⁻ ions are attracted, lose electrons (are oxidised), and form fluorine gas.

(ii) Write the ionic half-equation for the reaction at the negative electrode (cathode). [1]

Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na

Recap

Ionic half-equations show the transfer of electrons. At the cathode (negative electrode), reduction always occurs, which is the gain of electrons (OIL RIG: Reduction Is Gain). The positive sodium ion (Na⁺) gains one electron (e⁻) to become a neutral sodium atom (Na).

(e) Complete the dot-and-cross diagram to show the electron arrangement in a molecule of fluorine, F₂.

Show the outer electrons only. [1]

The completed diagram would show:

  • A single shared pair of electrons between the two fluorine atoms (one dot and one cross).
  • Each fluorine atom also has 6 non-bonding electrons (3 lone pairs) in its outer shell.
  • This gives both atoms a full octet (8 electrons) in their outer shell.

Recap

A covalent bond is formed when two non-metal atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. Fluorine is in Group VII, so it has 7 outer electrons. By sharing one electron with another fluorine atom, both atoms can achieve a stable, full outer shell of 8 electrons, like a noble gas. This is a single covalent bond.

explanation: The temperature of –195 °C is below the boiling point (–188 °C) but above the melting point (–220 °C).

Recap

A substance's physical state is determined by its temperature relative to its melting and boiling points.
- Below melting point = Solid

(ii) Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why sodium fluoride has a much higher melting point than fluorine. [3]

Sodium fluoride (NaF) is a giant ionic lattice structure. It has strong electrostatic forces of attraction (ionic bonds) between its oppositely charged ions (Na⁺ and F⁻).

Fluorine (F₂) has a simple molecular structure. It has weak forces of attraction, known as intermolecular forces, between its molecules.

A large amount of energy is needed to overcome the strong ionic bonds in NaF, but only a small amount of energy is needed to overcome the weak intermolecular forces in F₂. Therefore, NaF has a much higher melting point.

Recap

The melting point of a substance depends on the strength of the forces between its particles.
Giant Ionic Structures (e.g., NaF): Particles are ions. Forces are strong electrostatic attractions (ionic bonds). These require a lot of energy to break, resulting in high melting points.
Simple Molecular Structures (e.g., F₂): Particles are molecules. Forces *within* molecules (covalent bonds) are strong, but forces *between* molecules (intermolecular forces) are weak. Melting only requires overcoming these weak forces, which takes very little energy, resulting in low melting points.

Topics: Chemical Equilibria (Le Chatelier's Principle)

Hydrogen iodide, HI, decomposes into iodine and hydrogen. The reaction is reversible.

2HI(g) ⇌ I₂(g) + H₂(g)

colourless gas    purple gas    colourless gas

A gas syringe containing a mixture of hydrogen iodide, iodine and hydrogen gases was sealed. After reaching equilibrium the mixture was a pale purple colour.

(a) State what is meant by the term equilibrium. [2]

Equilibrium is reached in a reversible reaction when the rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the reverse reaction. At this point, the concentrations of the reactants and products are constant (in a closed system).

Recap

A reversible reaction (shown by the ⇋ symbol) is one where products can react to re-form the original reactants. For equilibrium to be established, the reaction must be in a closed system, meaning no substances can enter or leave.

(b) The plunger of the gas syringe is pushed in. The position of equilibrium does not change. The colour of the gaseous mixture turns darker purple.

The temperature remains constant.

(i) Explain why the position of equilibrium does not change. [1]

The position of equilibrium does not change because there is the same number of moles of gas on both sides of the equation (2 moles of HI on the left and 1 mole of I₂ + 1 mole of H₂, totalling 2 moles, on the right).

Recap

Le Chatelier's principle states that if a change in pressure is applied to a system at equilibrium, the equilibrium will shift to the side with fewer moles of gas to oppose the change. In this reaction, there are 2 moles of gas on the reactant side and 2 moles of gas on the product side. Since the number of moles is equal, a change in pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium.

(ii) Suggest why the colour of the gaseous mixture turns darker purple even though the position of equilibrium does not change. [1]

When the plunger is pushed in, the volume of the gas syringe decreases. This forces the iodine particles (molecules) closer together, increasing their concentration and making the purple colour appear darker.

Recap

The concentration of a gas is the number of particles in a given volume. Decreasing the volume (by pushing in the plunger) increases the concentration of all gases in the mixture, even if the *ratio* of products to reactants (the equilibrium position) remains the same.

(c) The forward reaction is endothermic.

(i) State what happens to the position of equilibrium when the temperature is decreased. [1]

The position of equilibrium shifts to the left (in favour of the reactants).

Recap

According to Le Chatelier's principle, if the temperature is decreased, the equilibrium will shift to oppose this change. It will favour the exothermic reaction (the one that releases heat). Since the forward reaction is endothermic, the reverse reaction must be exothermic. Therefore, the equilibrium shifts to the left.

(ii) State what happens to the rate of the forward reaction and the rate of the backward reaction when the temperature of the mixture is decreased. [2]

rate of the forward reaction ..............................................

rate of the backward reaction ..............................................

rate of the forward reaction: decreases (slower)

rate of the backward reaction: decreases (slower)

Recap

A decrease in temperature decreases the rate of *all* reactions (both forward and reverse). This is because the particles have less kinetic energy, resulting in fewer collisions per second. More importantly, fewer of these collisions will have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy (fewer successful collisions).

(a) Salts that are soluble in water can be made by the reaction between insoluble carbonates and dilute acids. Zinc sulfate is soluble in water.

You are provided with solid zinc carbonate, ZnCO₃, and dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄.

Describe how you would make a pure sample of zinc sulfate crystals.

Your answer should include:

  • practical details
  • how you would make sure that all the dilute sulfuric acid has reacted
  • a chemical equation for the reaction.

Practical Details:

  1. Add the solid zinc carbonate to the dilute sulfuric acid, stirring gently, until no more dissolves. You will know the acid has all reacted because the solid zinc carbonate is in excess and you will see no more effervescence (fizzing).
  2. Filter the mixture to remove the excess (unreacted) zinc carbonate, which is the residue. The filtrate is the zinc sulfate solution.
  3. Gently heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water until the solution becomes saturated (the point of crystallisation).
  4. Allow the saturated solution to cool slowly at room temperature, which will cause crystals of zinc sulfate to form.
  5. Filter off the crystals and dry them, for example, by pressing them between two pieces of filter paper.

Chemical Equation:

ZnCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + CO₂ + H₂O

Recap

This is the standard method for preparing a soluble salt from an insoluble base or carbonate. The key steps are: 1. React the acid with an excess of the solid. 2. Filter to remove the excess solid. 3. Crystallise the salt from the filtrate by evaporating some solvent and cooling. 4. Filter and dry the crystals.

(ii) Complete the equation for the decomposition of hydrated iron(II) sulfate. [2]

.....FeSO₄·7H₂O → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃ + .....H₂O

2FeSO₄·7H₂O → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃ + 14H₂O

Recap

To balance an equation, you must have the same number of each atom on both sides.
1. Fe: There are 2 Fe on the right (in Fe₂O₃), so we need 2 FeSO₄·7H₂O on the left.
2. S: This gives 2 S on the left, which balances the 1 S in SO₂ and 1 S in SO₃ on the right.
