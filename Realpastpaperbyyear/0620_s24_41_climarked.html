Question 1 | Paper: 0620/41/M/J/24 Q1 | Topics: Chemical Processes, Separation Techniques

Name the process that is used to:

(a) convert sulfur dioxide into sulfur trioxide in the manufacture of sulfuric acid [1]

The process is the Contact process.

Recap

The Contact process is the industrial method for making sulfuric acid. It involves using sulfur dioxide (from burning sulfur or roasting sulfide ores) and oxygen to make sulfur trioxide, using a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst.

(b) obtain water from aqueous sodium chloride [1]

The process is distillation.

Recap

Simple distillation is a purification method used to separate a liquid solvent (like water) from dissolved solids (like sodium chloride). The process involves boiling the liquid to form a vapour, and then condensing the vapour back into a pure liquid.

(c) extract aluminium from purified bauxite [1]

The process is electrolysis.

Recap

Bauxite is the main ore of aluminium, containing aluminium oxide. Aluminium is a reactive metal, placing it above carbon in the reactivity series. Metals that are more reactive than carbon must be extracted from their molten oxides using electrolysis.

(d) separate petroleum into useful substances [1]

The process is fractional distillation.

Recap

Petroleum is a mixture of hydrocarbons. It is separated into groups of molecules called fractions using fractional distillation. This process works because the different hydrocarbons have different chain lengths, which results in them having different boiling points.

(e) produce ethanol from aqueous glucose [1]

The process is fermentation.

Recap

Fermentation is the process where glucose is broken down by yeast to produce ethanol and carbon dioxide. This biological process must occur in the absence of air (anaerobic conditions) and at a warm temperature, typically 25–35 °C.

(f) manufacture alkenes and hydrogen from large alkane molecules [1]

The process is cracking.

Recap

Cracking is the thermal decomposition of long-chain alkanes into shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes. This process requires a high temperature and a catalyst. Hydrogen can also be produced during cracking. This is done because there is a higher demand for shorter-chain alkanes (like petrol) and alkenes (for making polymers) than for the larger fractions.

(g) separate a mixture of soluble coloured substances. [1]

The process is chromatography.

Recap

Paper chromatography is a technique used to separate a mixture of soluble coloured substances, such as inks or dyes. The separation depends on how soluble each substance is in the solvent and how strongly it adheres to the paper, causing the different substances to travel up the paper at different rates.

Recap

Here are the rules for determining the particles in an atom or ion:

  • Proton Number (Atomic Number): This is the number of protons in the nucleus. It defines the element. It is the bottom number in the ${}_{Z}^{A}X$ notation (e.g., 29 in ${}_{29}^{63}Cu^{+}$).
  • Mass Number (Nucleon Number): This is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. It is the top number (e.g., 37 in 37Cl).
  • Number of Neutrons: This is calculated as Mass Number - Proton Number.
    • For 37Cl: 37 - 17 = 20 neutrons.
  • Number of Electrons in a neutral atom: In a neutral atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons.
    • For 37Cl (atom): 17 protons = 17 electrons.
  • Number of Electrons in an ion:
    • Positive ions (cations) have lost electrons. The charge indicates how many. For ${}_{29}^{63}Cu^{+}$: It has 29 protons, but a +1 charge, so it has lost 1 electron. 29 - 1 = 28 electrons.
    • Negative ions (anions) have gained electrons. For the last row: It has 16 protons but 18 electrons, meaning it has gained 2 electrons. Its charge is 2-. The element with 16 protons is Sulfur (S). Its mass number is protons + neutrons (16 + 17 = 33). Therefore, the ion is ${}_{16}^{33}S^{2-}$.
Question 3 | Paper: 0620/41/M/J/24 Q3 | Topics: Bonding, Structure, Electrolysis

This question is about the elements sodium and fluorine and the compound sodium fluoride.

(a) Sodium reacts with fluorine to form sodium fluoride.
Write a symbol equation for this reaction. [2]

2Na + F₂ → 2NaF

Recap

Sodium (Na) is a Group I metal, so it forms a +1 ion (Na⁺). Fluorine (F) is a Group VII halogen. Halogens are diatomic (exist as F₂) and form -1 ions (F⁻). To form the neutral ionic compound sodium fluoride, one Na⁺ ion bonds with one F⁻ ion, giving the formula NaF. To balance the equation, two sodium atoms are needed to react with one fluorine molecule: 2Na + F₂ → 2NaF.

(b) Some properties of sodium, fluorine and sodium fluoride are shown in Table 3.1.

(i) Explain why sodium conducts electricity when it is a solid. [1]

Sodium has delocalised electrons that are mobile and free to move throughout the solid metallic lattice, carrying charge.

Recap

Metallic bonding consists of a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a 'sea' of delocalised electrons. These delocalised electrons are not fixed to any single atom and are mobile. When a voltage is applied, these electrons can flow through the structure, carrying an electric current. This is why metals are good electrical conductors.

(ii) Complete the dot-and-cross diagram in Fig. 3.1 of a molecule of fluorine.
Show outer shell electrons only. [2]

The completed diagram shows one shared pair of electrons (one dot, one cross) between the two fluorine atoms. Each fluorine atom also has 6 non-bonding electrons (3 pairs) in its outer shell, completing the octet for both atoms.

Recap

A single covalent bond is formed when two non-metal atoms share a pair of electrons. Fluorine is in Group VII, so it has 7 outer shell electrons. To achieve a stable noble gas configuration (a full outer shell of 8 electrons), two fluorine atoms each share one electron with each other, forming one shared pair and a diatomic F₂ molecule.

(iii) Deduce the physical state of fluorine at –200 °C. Use the data in Table 3.1 to explain your answer. [2]

Physical state: Liquid

Explanation: The temperature of –200 °C is above fluorine's melting point of –220 °C (so it is not a solid) but below its boiling point of –188 °C (so it is not a gas).

Recap

The physical state of a substance at a given temperature can be deduced from its melting and boiling points:

  • Below the melting point, the substance is a solid.
  • Between the melting point and boiling point, the substance is a liquid.
  • Above the boiling point, the substance is a gas.
Remember that with negative numbers, –200 is a *higher* temperature than –220.

(iv) Explain in terms of structure and bonding why sodium fluoride has a much higher melting point than fluorine. [3]

Sodium fluoride (NaF) has a giant ionic lattice structure with strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions (Na⁺ and F⁻).

Fluorine (F₂) has a simple molecular structure. While the covalent bonds within the F₂ molecules are strong, the forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces) are weak.

A large amount of energy is needed to overcome the strong ionic bonds in NaF, but only a small amount of energy is needed to overcome the weak intermolecular forces in F₂. Therefore, NaF has a much higher melting point.

Recap

The melting and boiling points of a substance depend on the strength of the forces between its particles.

  • Ionic compounds (like NaF) form giant ionic lattices. They have very high melting points because a lot of energy is needed to break the strong electrostatic forces (ionic bonds) between the many ions.
  • Simple molecular substances (like F₂) have low melting points. This is because only the weak intermolecular forces of attraction *between* the molecules need to be overcome, which requires very little energy.

(c) Dilute aqueous sodium fluoride undergoes electrolysis. Hydrogen is produced at the cathode.

(i) State what is meant by the term electrolysis. [2]

Electrolysis is the decomposition (breakdown) of an ionic compound, when it is molten or in an aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

Recap

An electrolyte is a molten or aqueous ionic compound that conducts electricity and is decomposed by it. Electrolysis is the process of using electricity to break down this compound. The substance must be molten or dissolved so that its ions are free to move and carry charge.

(ii) Write an ionic half-equation for the production of hydrogen at the cathode. [2]

2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

Recap

During the electrolysis of an *aqueous* solution, water (H₂O) partially ionises to form H⁺ and OH⁻ ions.

  • At the cathode (negative electrode), positive ions are attracted. The H⁺ ions from water are less reactive than the Na⁺ ions from sodium fluoride.
  • Therefore, the H⁺ ions are discharged. They gain electrons (reduction) to form hydrogen gas.
  • The ionic half-equation is 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g).

Question 4 | Paper: 0620/41/M/J/24 Q4 | Topics: Chemical Equilibrium, Rates of Reaction

Hydrogen iodide thermally decomposes into iodine and hydrogen. The reaction is reversible.

2HI(g) ⇌ I₂(g) + H₂(g)
colourless gas   purple gas   colourless gas

Fig. 4.1 shows a gas syringe containing a mixture of hydrogen iodide, iodine and hydrogen gases. The gas syringe is sealed and the mixture is heated to 300 °C. The mixture of gases reaches equilibrium and is purple.

(a) State what is meant by the term equilibrium. [2]

A state of equilibrium is reached in a reversible reaction when the rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the reverse reaction. At this point, the concentrations of the reactants and products remain constant.

Recap

Equilibrium can only be reached in a closed system (like the sealed gas syringe). It is a dynamic state, meaning both the forward and reverse reactions are still happening, but they are occurring at the same rate, so there is no overall change in the amounts of each substance.

(b) The pressure of the mixture is increased. All other conditions stay the same. The position of equilibrium does not change. The colour of the gaseous mixture turns darker purple. The temperature remains constant.

2HI(g) ⇌ I₂(g) + H₂(g)

(i) Explain why the position of equilibrium does not change. [1]

The position of equilibrium does not change because there are an equal number of moles of gas on both sides of the equation (2 moles on the left and 1 + 1 = 2 moles on the right).

Recap

According to Le Chatelier's Principle, increasing the pressure will shift the equilibrium to the side with fewer moles of gas. In this reaction (2HI(g) ⇌ I₂(g) + H₂(g)), there are 2 moles of gas on the reactant side and 2 moles of gas on the product side. Since the number of moles is equal, a change in pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium.

(ii) Suggest why the colour of the mixture of gases turns darker purple. [1]

Increasing the pressure pushes all the gas particles closer together into a smaller volume. This increases the concentration of all gases, including the purple iodine (I₂) gas, making the colour appear darker.

Recap

The colour of a gaseous mixture depends on the concentration of the coloured substance. Even if the *position of equilibrium* doesn't shift, increasing the pressure on a gas mixture always increases the concentration of all components (same number of particles in less space). A higher concentration of purple I₂ gas results in a darker purple colour.

(c) The temperature of the mixture of gases is decreased. All other conditions stay the same. The mixture of gases turns lighter purple.
State what can be deduced about the forward reaction from this information. [1]

The forward reaction is endothermic.

Recap

Here's the logic:

  1. The mixture turns lighter purple, which means the concentration of purple I₂ has decreased.
  2. This means the equilibrium has shifted to the left (the reverse reaction is favoured).
  3. The change made was a decrease in temperature.
  4. Le Chatelier's Principle states that if you decrease the temperature, the equilibrium will shift in the exothermic (heat-releasing) direction to oppose the change.
  5. Since the equilibrium shifted left, the reverse reaction must be exothermic.
  6. Therefore, the forward reaction (2HI → I₂ + H₂) must be endothermic (it absorbs heat).

(d) Deduce the oxidation number of iodine, I, in: [2]
HI
I₂

In HI: -1
In I₂: 0

Recap

Oxidation numbers are assigned using a set of rules:

  • The oxidation number of an element (like I₂ or Na) is always 0.
  • The total oxidation number of all elements in a neutral compound (like HI) is 0.
  • In most compounds, hydrogen has an oxidation number of +1.
  • Therefore, in HI, to balance the +1 from hydrogen, the iodine (I) must have an oxidation number of -1.

(e) Methanol is manufactured by reacting carbon monoxide with hydrogen.

CO(g) + 2H₂(g) ⇌ CH₃OH(g)

Draw a circle around your chosen answer.

Recap

Transition elements (the block of metals between Groups II and III) and their compounds are often used as catalysts. Of the options listed, copper is the only transition element. The other elements are main group metals (calcium, sodium) or non-metals (carbon, sulfur).

(ii) State the effect on the position of equilibrium when a catalyst is used. [1]

A catalyst has no effect on the position of equilibrium.

Recap

A catalyst speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount. This means the system reaches equilibrium *faster*, but the final position of equilibrium (the amount of reactants and products at equilibrium) is not changed.

(iii) State the effect that a catalyst has on the activation energy, Eₐ, of a reaction. [1]

A catalyst lowers the activation energy (Eₐ).

Recap

A catalyst works by providing an alternative reaction pathway that requires less energy. This "less energy" is the lower activation energy (Eₐ). By lowering this energy barrier, a greater proportion of particle collisions have sufficient energy to react, thus increasing the rate of reaction.

Question 5 | Paper: 0620/41/M/J/24 Q5 | Topics: Salts, Qualitative Analysis, Stoichiometry

(a) Lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂, is an insoluble salt and is made by precipitation.

(i) Name two aqueous solutions that produce a precipitate of lead(II) bromide when they are mixed. [2]

  1. An aqueous solution of a soluble lead(II) salt, such as lead(II) nitrate.
  2. An aqueous solution of a soluble bromide salt, such as sodium bromide (or potassium bromide).

Recap

To make an insoluble salt (like PbBr₂) by precipitation, you must mix two soluble solutions.

  • One solution must contain the cation (Pb²⁺). According to solubility rules, all nitrates are soluble, so lead(II) nitrate (Pb(NO₃)₂) is a good choice.
  • The other solution must contain the anion (Br⁻). All Group I salts (like sodium, potassium) and ammonium salts are soluble, so sodium bromide (NaBr) or potassium bromide (KBr) are good choices.

(ii) Describe how to produce a pure sample of lead(II) bromide from the mixture of aqueous solutions in (a)(i). [2]

First, filter the mixture to separate the solid precipitate (the residue) from the solution. Then, wash the residue on the filter paper with distilled water to remove any soluble impurities. Finally, dry the solid, for example by pressing it between filter papers or placing it in a low-temperature oven.

Recap

The preparation of an insoluble salt by precipitation involves three key steps after mixing:

  1. Filtration: To separate the solid precipitate (residue) from the filtrate.
  2. Washing: Using distilled water to rinse the precipitate. This removes any soluble spectator ions left on its surface.
  3. Drying: To remove the water, often done by leaving in a warm oven (not too hot) or pressing between filter papers.

(iii) Write an ionic equation for the precipitation reaction which produces lead(II) bromide. Include state symbols. [3]

Pb²⁺(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → PbBr₂(s)

Recap

An ionic equation shows only the species that change during the reaction. In the reaction Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2NaBr(aq) → PbBr₂(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq), the Na⁺(aq) and NO₃⁻(aq) ions are spectator ions—they start as aqueous ions and end as aqueous ions.
The only species that react are the lead(II) ions and the bromide ions, which come together to form the solid precipitate. Remember to include state symbols and balance the charges (one Pb²⁺ reacts with two Br⁻).

(d) Hydrated cobalt(II) sulfate, CoSO₄•xH₂O, produces water when it is heated.
CoSO₄•xH₂O(s) → CoSO₄(s) + xH₂O(g)

(i) Describe what else the student should do to ensure that all the water has been given off. No other substances are required. [2]

The student should heat the remaining solid again, allow it to cool, and then weigh it again. This process of heating and reweighing should be repeated until the mass becomes constant (stops decreasing), which indicates that all the water has been driven off.

Recap

This experimental technique is called 'heating to constant mass'. It is the only way to be sure that a decomposition reaction (especially one involving the loss of water or another gas) is complete. If the mass continues to drop after reheating, it means there was still water present.

(ii) In an experiment, 1.405 g of CoSO₄•xH₂O is heated until all the water is given off. The mass of CoSO₄ that remains is 0.775 g.
[Mᵣ: CoSO₄, 155; H₂O, 18]
Determine the value of x using the following steps. [4]

Calculate the number of moles of CoSO₄ that remains.
Moles = Mass / Mᵣ = 0.775 g / 155 g/mol = 0.005 mol

Calculate the mass of H₂O given off.
Mass H₂O = Initial mass - Final mass = 1.405 g - 0.775 g = 0.63 g

Calculate the number of moles of H₂O given off.
Moles = Mass / Mᵣ = 0.63 g / 18 g/mol = 0.035 mol

Determine the value of x.
To find the ratio x, divide the moles of water by the moles of the anhydrous salt:
Ratio = Moles H₂O / Moles CoSO₄ = 0.035 mol / 0.005 mol = 7
x = 7

Recap

The formula CoSO₄•xH₂O represents a molar ratio. It means 'for every 1 mole of CoSO₄, there are x moles of H₂O'.
To find this ratio from experimental masses:

  1. Calculate the mass of the anhydrous salt (CoSO₄) and the mass of the water lost.
  2. Convert both of these masses into moles using the formula: moles = mass / Mᵣ.
  3. Divide the moles of water by the moles of the anhydrous salt to find the simplest whole-number ratio, x.

Question 6 | Paper: 0620/41/M/J/24 Q6 | Topics: Metal Extraction, Reactivity, Rusting

(a) Fig. 6.1 shows a blast furnace used to extract iron from its ore.

(i) Coke and iron ore are added at the top of the blast furnace.
Name one other substance that is added at the top of the blast furnace. [1]

Limestone (or calcium carbonate).

Recap

The three solid raw materials added to the top of the blast furnace are:

  1. Iron ore (e.g., hematite, Fe₂O₃) - the source of the iron.
  2. Coke (carbon) - acts as the fuel and produces the reducing agent.
  3. Limestone (calcium carbonate, CaCO₃) - used to remove acidic impurities.
Hot air (oxygen) is blown in at the bottom.

(ii) Name the substance that leaves the blast furnace at A. [1]

Molten iron.

Recap

Inside the furnace, two molten layers are formed. The molten iron is denser, so it collects at the very bottom (tapped off at A). The molten slag is less dense and floats on top of the iron (tapped off at the 'slag' exit).

(iii) Slag is produced from an impurity in iron ore.
Name the impurity in iron ore that is converted into slag. [1]

Silicon(IV) oxide (or silicon dioxide, SiO₂).

Recap

Limestone (CaCO₃) is added to remove the main impurity, silicon(IV) oxide (SiO₂), which is acidic.

  1. The limestone thermally decomposes: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
  2. The basic calcium oxide (CaO) then neutralises the acidic SiO₂: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (calcium silicate, or molten slag).

(iv) Name two substances that react together to produce the high temperature in the blast furnace. [1]

Carbon (from coke) and oxygen (from the hot air).

Recap

The blast furnace reaches temperatures over 1500 °C. This intense heat is generated by the exothermic reaction of coke (carbon) burning in the hot oxygen blown into the furnace: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g). This reaction provides the heat for all the other reactions.

(v) Name two waste gases that leave the blast furnace. [2]

Any two from: Nitrogen (N₂), Carbon dioxide (CO₂), or Argon (Ar).

Recap

The main waste gases are:

  • Nitrogen: This makes up 78% of the air blown in and is unreactive, so it passes through the furnace and exits.
  • Carbon dioxide: Produced from the combustion of coke (C + O₂ → CO₂) and the reduction of iron ore (Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂).
  • (Carbon monoxide is also present, but it is a reactant. Argon is present as ~1% of the air and is also unreactive).

(b) Zinc is produced from zinc oxide in a furnace. The zinc is produced as a gas. It then forms molten zinc.

(i) Suggest why the zinc produced inside the furnace is a gas. [1]

The temperature inside the furnace is above the boiling point of zinc.

Recap

Substances are in the gaseous state when their temperature is above their boiling point. Zinc's boiling point (907 °C) is lower than the operating temperature of the reduction furnace, so it vaporises as soon as it is formed.

(ii) State the name of the physical change that occurs when gaseous zinc is converted into molten zinc. [1]

Condensation (or condensing).

Recap

The changes of state are:

  • Solid to Liquid: Melting
  • Liquid to Gas: Boiling / Evaporation
  • Gas to Liquid: Condensation
  • Liquid to Solid: Freezing / Solidifying

(c) Zinc is used to coat iron to prevent rusting.

(i) Name the process used to coat iron with zinc as a method of rust prevention. [1]

Galvanising.

Recap

Galvanising is the specific name for the process of coating iron or steel with a layer of zinc to protect it from rusting.

(ii) When the zinc coating is scratched, the iron underneath does not rust.
Explain why the iron underneath the zinc does not rust. [2]

This is called sacrificial protection. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it reacts with the air and water instead of the iron. The zinc corrodes (is oxidised, or loses electrons) preferentially, thereby protecting the iron.

Recap

Sacrificial protection works by pairing iron with a more reactive metal. For rusting (the corrosion of iron) to occur, iron must be oxidised (lose electrons): Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻.
Because zinc is more reactive, it loses electrons more easily than iron (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻). When the two metals are in contact, the zinc provides electrons to the iron, forcing the iron to remain as an un-oxidised atom, while the zinc corrodes in its place.

(d) Zinc oxide neutralises both acids and bases.

(i) State the general name given to oxides that neutralise both acids and bases. [1]

Amphoteric.

Recap

Oxides can be classified based on their reaction with acids and bases:

  • Basic oxides (most metal oxides, e.g., CaO) react with acids.
  • Acidic oxides (most non-metal oxides, e.g., SO₂) react with bases.
  • Amphoteric oxides (e.g., zinc oxide, ZnO, and aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃) react with both acids and strong bases.

(ii) When zinc oxide neutralises aqueous sodium hydroxide, sodium zincate is formed.
The formula of the zincate ion is ZnO₂²⁻.
Deduce the formula of sodium zincate. [1]

Na₂ZnO₂

Recap

To write the formula for an ionic compound, you must balance the charges of the ions.

  • The sodium ion (Group I) has a charge of 1+ (Na⁺).
  • The zincate ion is given as having a charge of 2- (ZnO₂²⁻).
To make a neutral compound, you need two Na⁺ ions to balance the one 2- charge of the zincate ion. This gives the formula Na₂ZnO₂.

Zinc sulfate.

Recap

A base (zinc oxide) reacts with an acid (sulfuric acid) in a neutralisation reaction to form a salt and water.
The name of the salt comes from the metal of the base (Zinc) and the non-metal part of the acid (sulfuric acid forms sulfate salts).

Question 7 | Paper: 0620/41/M/J/24 Q7 | Topics: Organic Chemistry, Stoichiometry, Polymers

Many organic compounds contain carbon and hydrogen only.

(a) (i) Organic compound A has the following composition by mass.
C, 82.76%; H, 17.24%
Calculate the empirical formula of compound A. [3]

Step 1: Divide percentage by relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) for each element (C=12, H=1).
C: 82.76 / 12 = 6.896...
H: 17.24 / 1 = 17.24

Step 2: Divide both results by the smallest number (6.896).
C: 6.896 / 6.896 = 1
H: 17.24 / 6.896 = 2.5

Step 3: Multiply to get the simplest whole number ratio.
C: 1 x 2 = 2
H: 2.5 x 2 = 5

Empirical formula = C₂H₅

Recap

The empirical formula is the simplest whole number ratio of atoms in a compound. To calculate it from percentage composition:

  1. Assume 100g of the substance, so percentages become masses (e.g., 82.76 g of C).
  2. Divide the mass of each element by its relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) to find the molar ratio.
  3. Divide all the molar ratios by the smallest ratio calculated in step 2.
  4. If the results are not whole numbers, multiply all of them by a small integer (like 2, 3, etc.) until they are all simple whole numbers.

(ii) Compound B has the empirical formula CH₂ and a relative molecular mass of 70.
Determine the molecular formula of compound B. [1]

Step 1: Calculate the mass of the empirical formula (CH₂).
Mass = (1 x 12) + (2 x 1) = 14

Step 2: Divide the relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) by the empirical formula mass.
n = 70 / 14 = 5

Step 3: Multiply the empirical formula by this number (n).
(CH₂) x 5 = C₅H₁₀

Molecular formula = C₅H₁₀

Recap

The molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. It is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. To find it, you divide the relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) by the empirical formula mass to find the multiplier, 'n'.
