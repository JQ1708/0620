Organic Field Guide

A guide to the families of organic compounds.

Origin: Fossil Fuels

Most organic compounds we use originate from crude oil (petroleum), a fossil fuel. Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons. We separate it into useful parts, called fractions, using a process called fractional distillation.

Homologous Series

Organic compounds are classified into families called homologous series. Each series has:

  • The same general formula.
  • Similar chemical properties due to the same functional group.
  • A gradual trend in physical properties (e.g., boiling point increases as chain length increases).
  • A different neighboring compound by a -CH₂- group.
Naming Prefixes

The start of an organic molecule's name depends on the number of carbon atoms in its longest chain.

  • Meth-: 1 Carbon
  • Eth-: 2 Carbons
  • Prop-: 3 Carbons
  • But-: 4 Carbons

Family: Alkanes

Saturated hydrocarbons with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. They only contain single C-C bonds and are generally unreactive.

Reaction 1: Combustion

Alkanes are excellent fuels. They undergo complete combustion with sufficient oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water.

Alkane + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water

Reaction 2: Substitution

Alkanes react with halogens (e.g., chlorine, bromine) in the presence of ultraviolet (UV) light. One hydrogen atom is swapped for a halogen atom.

Methane + Chlorine → Chloromethane + Hydrogen Chloride

Isomerism

From butane (C₄H₁₀) onwards, alkanes exhibit structural isomerism: molecules with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements.

Process: Cracking

Cracking is a process that breaks down large, less useful hydrocarbon molecules (long-chain alkanes) into smaller, more useful ones, including alkenes and hydrogen. This is achieved by passing the alkane vapor over a hot catalyst (e.g., silica or alumina) at high temperatures (~600-700°C).

Family: Alkenes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ. They contain at least one C=C double bond, making them more reactive than alkanes.

Key Reaction: Addition Reactions

The C=C double bond is a site of high reactivity, allowing alkenes to undergo addition reactions where the double bond breaks and new atoms are added across it.

  • Hydrogenation: Addition of hydrogen (H₂) with a nickel catalyst to form an alkane.
  • Hydration: Addition of steam (H₂O) with high temperature, pressure and a catalyst to form an alcohol.
  • Halogenation: Addition of halogens (e.g., Br₂, Cl₂) at room temperature.
Test for Unsaturation

Alkenes can decolourise bromine water, turning it from orange-brown to colourless. This is the chemical test to distinguish an unsaturated alkene from a saturated alkane.

Family: Alcohols

General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH. Their functional group is the hydroxyl group, -OH.

Production of Ethanol
  • Fermentation: Enzymes in yeast break down glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Conditions: Absence of oxygen, ~37°C. This is a batch process.
  • Hydration of Ethene: Ethene reacts with steam. Conditions: High temperature (~300°C), high pressure (~60-70 atm), phosphoric(V) acid catalyst. This is a continuous process.
Key Reactions
  • Combustion: Alcohols burn in excess oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water.
  • Oxidation: They can be oxidised by an oxidising agent (like potassium manganate(VII)) or by microbial action to produce carboxylic acids. This is how wine turns into vinegar.

Family: Carboxylic Acids

Their functional group is the carboxyl group, -COOH. They are formed from the oxidation of alcohols.

Acidic Properties

They are weak acids, meaning they only partially ionise in water. As acids, they react with:

  • Metals to produce a salt and hydrogen.
  • Carbonates to produce a salt, water, and carbon dioxide.

Family: Esters

Formed from an alcohol and a carboxylic acid in an esterification reaction (with an acid catalyst). Their functional group is -COO-.

Esters are known for their sweet, fruity smells and are used in perfumes and food flavourings.

Naming Esters

The name has two parts:

  • The first part comes from the alcohol and ends in -yl (e.g., from ethanol → ethyl).
  • The second part comes from the carboxylic acid and ends in -oate (e.g., from ethanoic acid → ethanoate).

Example: Ethanol + Ethanoic Acid → Ethyl ethanoate
