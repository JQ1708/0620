Cyclopropane is a colourless gas.

Cyclopropane reacts with bromine at room temperature. The chemical equation for the reaction is shown.

(a) (i) What is the empirical formula of cyclopropane? [1]

Tap to reveal answer

CH₂

(ii) What colour change, if any, would you see when cyclopropane is bubbled into aqueous bromine? [2]

initial colour

final colour

Tap to reveal answer

final colour: colourless

(b) The reaction of cyclopropane with bromine is exothermic.

(i) Complete the energy level diagram for this reaction by

  • adding the product of the reaction,
  • labelling the energy change, ΔH
Tap to reveal answer

An approximately horizontal line should be drawn to the right of and below the reactant line. The energy change, ΔH, should be shown as a downward arrow from the reactant energy level to the product energy level.

(ii) Propene also reacts with bromine.

Use the bond energies in the table to calculate the energy change, ΔH, for the reaction. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

Energy required to break bonds (reactants): (1 × C=C) + (1 × Br-Br) = 611 + 193 = 804 kJ/mol

Energy given out when bonds made (products): (1 × C-C) + (2 × C-Br) = 348 + (2 × 285) = 918 kJ/mol

Energy change = Energy in - Energy out = 804 - 918 = -114 kJ/mol

(c) The boiling point of bromine is 59 °C and the boiling point of iodine is 184 °C.

Tap to reveal answer

Iodine has stronger intermolecular forces of attraction between its molecules than bromine. More energy is needed to overcome these forces.

Question 3 (0620/41_Summer_2017_Q3) - Separating Substances, Using Moles, Reacting Masses And Chemical Equations

Magnesium sulfate and lead(II) sulfate are examples of salts.

(a) A student prepared magnesium sulfate crystals starting from magnesium carbonate. The student carried out the experiment in four steps.

step 1 The student added excess magnesium carbonate to a small volume of dilute sulfuric acid until no more magnesium carbonate would react.

step 2 The student filtered the mixture.

step 3 The student heated the filtrate obtained from step 2 until it was saturated.

step 4 The student allowed the hot filtrate to cool to room temperature and then removed the crystals which formed.

(i) How did the student know when the reaction had finished in step 1? [1]

Tap to reveal answer

There was no more effervescence / fizzing.

(ii) Name the residue in step 2. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Magnesium carbonate.

(iii) A saturated solution forms in step 3. What is a saturated solution? [2]

Tap to reveal answer

A solution in which no more solute will dissolve at a particular temperature.

(iv) Explain why magnesium sulfate crystals form during step 4. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

The solubility of magnesium sulfate decreases as the temperature decreases.

(b) Magnesium sulfate crystals are hydrated. Another student heated some hydrated magnesium sulfate crystals in a crucible and obtained the following results.

mass of hydrated magnesium sulfate crystals = 4.92 g

mass of water removed = 2.52 g

(i) Calculate the number of moles of water removed. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Moles of water = mass ÷ Mᵣ = 2.52 ÷ 18 = 0.14 mol.

(ii) Calculate the number of moles of anhydrous magnesium sulfate remaining in the crucible. The Mᵣ of anhydrous magnesium sulfate is 120. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Mass of anhydrous MgSO₄ = 4.92 - 2.52 = 2.40 g.

Moles of MgSO₄ = mass ÷ Mᵣ = 2.40 ÷ 120 = 0.02 mol.

(iii) Calculate the ratio of moles of anhydrous magnesium sulfate : moles of water. Give your answer as whole numbers. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Ratio = 0.02 : 0.14. Divide by smallest: (0.02 ÷ 0.02) : (0.14 ÷ 0.02) = 1 : 7.

(iv) Suggest the formula of hydrated magnesium sulfate crystals. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

MgSO₄·7H₂O

(c) Lead(II) sulfate, PbSO₄, is insoluble.

Describe how you would prepare a pure dry sample of lead(II) sulfate crystals starting from solutions of lead(II) nitrate and sodium sulfate. Include a series of key steps in your answer. [4]

Tap to reveal answer

1. Mix the two solutions (lead(II) nitrate and sodium sulfate).

2. Filter the mixture to separate the precipitate (lead(II) sulfate).

3. Wash the residue with distilled water.

4. Dry the residue, for example, in a warm oven or between sheets of filter paper.

(d) Write the ionic equation for the reaction which takes place between solutions of lead(II) nitrate and sodium sulfate. Include state symbols. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Pb²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → PbSO₄(s)

Question 5 (0620/41_Summer_2017_Q5) - Separating Substances, The Speed Of A Reaction, Reacting Masses And Chemical Equations

When barium carbonate is added to dilute hydrochloric acid, carbon dioxide gas is formed.

A student carried out an experiment to measure the volume of gas formed as a reaction proceeds. The student added a small mass of powdered barium carbonate to an excess of 0.1 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. A graph of the results was drawn. The graph is shown.

(a) Name the two pieces of apparatus needed to take the measurements shown on the graph. [1]

1

2

Tap to reveal answer

1. Gas syringe (to measure volume of gas).

2. Stop-watch or stop-clock (to measure time).

(b) On the axes below, sketch a graph to show how the rate of reaction changes as the reaction proceeds. Assume the initial rate of reaction is represented by the point at X. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

The graph should start at point X and be a curve with a decreasing gradient, eventually reaching a rate of zero when the reaction stops (at approx. 114 s).

(c) The total volume of gas collected was 180 cm³ at room temperature and pressure. Calculate the mass, in grams, of barium carbonate used. [3]

BaCO₃ + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Tap to reveal answer

1. Moles of CO₂ = volume ÷ 24000 = 180 ÷ 24000 = 0.0075 mol.

2. Mole ratio of BaCO₃ : CO₂ is 1:1, so moles of BaCO₃ = 0.0075 mol.

3. Mᵣ of BaCO₃ = 137 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 197.

4. Mass of BaCO₃ = moles × Mᵣ = 0.0075 × 197 = 1.48 g.

(d) The original graph has been drawn again. On the grid, draw the graph expected if the same mass of barium carbonate is added as large lumps instead of as a powder. All other conditions are the same as in the original experiment. Explain why your graph is different from the original graph. [2]

Tap to reveal answer

Graph: The new curve starts from (0,0), has a less steep gradient (slower rate) than the original, but reaches the same final volume of 180 cm³.

Explanation: The large lumps have a smaller surface area than the powder, leading to a lower frequency of successful collisions and a slower reaction rate.

(e) The original graph has been drawn again. On the grid, draw the graph expected if the concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid is changed from 0.1 mol/dm³ to 0.2 mol/dm³. All other conditions are the same as in the original experiment. Explain, in terms of particles, why your graph is different from the original graph. [4]

Tap to reveal answer

Graph: The new curve starts from (0,0), has a steeper gradient (faster rate) than the original, but reaches the same final volume of 180 cm³ (since BaCO₃ is the limiting reactant).

Explanation: The higher concentration means there are more acid particles per unit volume. This leads to more frequent collisions between reacting particles, so the rate of reaction increases.

(f) The experiment is changed and the mass of powdered barium carbonate is doubled. All other conditions are the same as in the original experiment. The acid is still in excess. Deduce the volume of gas formed at room temperature and pressure, in cm³, in this experiment. [1]

Tap to reveal answer

Since the mass of the limiting reactant (barium carbonate) is doubled, the moles of product will also double. Volume = 180 cm³ × 2 = 360 cm³.

Question 8 (0620/43_Winter_2018_Q4) - Using Moles, Separating Substances, In The Lab (chemical Test & Salt Analysis)

(a) Dilute sulfuric acid and aqueous potassium hydroxide can be used to make potassium sulfate crystals using a method that includes titration.

A student titrated 25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol/dm³ aqueous potassium hydroxide with dilute sulfuric acid in the presence of an indicator. The volume of dilute sulfuric acid needed to neutralise the aqueous potassium hydroxide was 20.0 cm³.

The equation for the reaction is shown.

H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

Determine the concentration of the dilute sulfuric acid. [3]

Tap to reveal answer

1. Moles of KOH = conc × vol = 0.0500 × (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00125 mol.

2. Moles of H₂SO₄ = Moles of KOH ÷ 2 = 0.00125 ÷ 2 = 0.000625 mol.

3. Concentration of H₂SO₄ = moles ÷ vol = 0.000625 ÷ (20.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.03125 mol/dm³.

(b) After the titration has been completed, the conical flask contains an aqueous solution of potassium sulfate and some of the dissolved indicator.

Describe how to prepare a pure, dry sample of potassium sulfate crystals from new solutions of dilute sulfuric acid and aqueous potassium hydroxide of the same concentrations as used in the titration. Include a series of key steps in your answer. [5]

Tap to reveal answer

1. Repeat the titration using the same volumes of acid and alkali, but without the indicator.

2. Heat the resulting solution to evaporate some of the water until the solution is saturated.

3. Allow the solution to cool slowly to form crystals.

4. Filter the crystals from the solution.

5. Dry the crystals, for example, by patting them between filter papers.

Tap to reveal answer

Write chemical equations for the reaction of dilute sulfuric acid with each of the following:
