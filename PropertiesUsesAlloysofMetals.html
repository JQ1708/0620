1. Properties of Metals

Let's start with the basics. The Periodic Table divides elements into two main types: metals and non-metals. Most elements are metals! There are also a few metalloids (or semimetals) that have properties of both. The metallic character diminishes moving from left to right across the Periodic Table.

What Makes a Metal a Metal?

Metals share a set of unique physical properties. Flip the cards below to find out what they are and why they happen!

Conductivity

(Tap to reveal why)

Metals conduct heat and electricity because they have delocalised electrons that are free to move through the structure and carry charge or thermal energy.

High Melting & Boiling Points

(Tap to reveal why)

Metals have strong forces of attraction in their metallic bonds. A large amount of energy is needed to break these bonds.

Malleable & Ductile

(Tap to reveal why)

The layers of positive ions in a metal can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bond. This allows them to be hammered into shape (malleable) or drawn into wires (ductile).

Other Key Properties:

  • High Density: The particles in metals are packed closely together.
  • Lustrous: They are shiny when cut or polished.
  • Sonorous: They make a ringing sound when struck.
  • Solid at Room Temperature: All metals are solid at room temperature, with one famous exception... Mercury!

2. Uses of Metals

A metal's job is determined by its unique skills! The properties of a metal are directly linked to its uses. Let's look at a few star players.

✈️

Aluminium

  • Properties: Low density and is highly resistant to corrosion.
  • Uses: Its low density makes it perfect for building aeroplanes and overhead electrical cables. Its corrosion resistance makes it great for food containers.
🔌

Copper

  • Properties: An excellent conductor of electricity and is very ductile (can be drawn into wires). It's also a good conductor of heat and quite unreactive.
  • Uses: The go-to metal for electrical wiring. Also used for water pipes due to its heat conductivity and unreactivity.
🏗️

Steel

  • Note: Steel is actually an alloy of iron, which we'll cover next!
  • Property: Incredibly strong.
  • Uses: The backbone of modern construction, used for buildings, bridges, and cars.

3. Alloys

What is an Alloy?

An alloy is a mixture containing a metal and at least one other element, which can be a metal or a non-metal. Think of it as a metallic recipe! For example, steel is an alloy of iron mixed with carbon and other elements.

Why Bother with Alloys?

Alloys often have enhanced properties that are more useful than those of pure metals. They can be:

  • Stronger
  • Harder
  • Resistant to corrosion/extreme temperatures

The Structure of an Alloy (Extended Tier)

The secret to an alloy's strength lies in its structure. Pure metals have a very regular arrangement of ions. Specifically, they are layers of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. Alloys, on the other hand, are irregular because they contain atoms of different sizes.

This distortion is key! The different sized atoms make it more difficult for the layers of ions to slide over each other. This is why alloys are generally harder and stronger than pure metals.

💡 Examiner's Tip!

Remember, alloys are mixtures, not compounds! In an exam, if you're asked to identify a diagram of an alloy, look for the one with uneven-sized particles and distorted layers.
