Experimental Techniques

Apparatus for Measurements

Time

Measured with a stopwatch or stopclock.

  • Usually accurate to one or two decimal places.
  • Units are typically seconds or minutes.
  • Other units may be used for extremely slow reactions (e.g. rusting).
  • 1 minute = 60 seconds.

Examiner Tip: Be careful with time units! A stopwatch showing 1:30 is 90 seconds or 1.5 minutes, NOT 1.30 minutes. To avoid confusion, it is best to use seconds.

Temperature

Measured with a thermometer or digital temperature probe.

  • Lab thermometers usually have a precision of 0.5°C or 1°C.
  • Digital probes have a higher resolution.

Mass

Measured with a digital balance.

  • Usually give readings to two decimal places.

Volume

Different apparatus can be used to measure volume, depending on the level of accuracy needed.

Measuring Cylinder

Good for approximate volumes. Read from the bottom of the meniscus.

Pipette

Used to measure fixed, accurate volumes of liquid (e.g., 25.0 cm³).

Burette

Used to measure variable, accurate volumes of liquid. Scale is read downwards from 0.0 at the top.

Gas Syringe

Used to measure the volume of gas produced in a reaction.

Beaker

For approximate measurements only; mainly used for mixing and heating.

Conical Flask

For approximate measurements; sloped sides prevent splashing, useful for titrations.

Volumetric Flask

Used to prepare standard solutions of a fixed, accurate volume.

Examiner Tip: Choosing the right apparatus is crucial. For preparing a 250 cm³ standard solution, a volumetric flask is required. For a titration, a pipette and burette are needed. For collecting a gas, a gas syringe is used. A measuring cylinder is only for approximate volumes.

Solutions

Solute

The solid substance that has dissolved in a liquid to form a solution.

Solvent

The liquid that dissolves the solid solute. Water is the most common solvent.

Solution

Formed when a solute dissolves in a solvent.

Concentration

Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in a certain volume of the solvent.

It is usually measured in g/dm³ or mol/dm³.

You can calculate concentration using the following formula:

Concentration = Amount of Solute (g or mol)⁄Volume of Solution (dm³)

Acid-Base Titrations

What is a Titration?

A titration is a technique used to find the exact volume of an acid (or alkali) needed to neutralise an alkali (or acid) of a known concentration.

Method

  1. Use a pipette to measure a known volume of one solution (e.g., alkali) into a conical flask. Place the flask on a white tile.
  2. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator to the flask.
  3. Fill a burette with the other solution (e.g., acid) and record the initial reading. Ensure the jet space is full.
  4. Add the solution from the burette to the flask, swirling, until the indicator changes colour permanently. This is the endpoint.
  5. Record the final burette reading. The volume added is the final reading minus the initial reading. This is the titre.
  6. The first titration is a rough trial to get an approximate endpoint.
  7. Repeat the titration accurately, adding the solution dropwise near the endpoint, until you get two results that are concordant (within 0.10 cm³ of each other).

Sources of Error

  • Not removing the funnel from the burette: Extra drops can fall in, causing a false/low titre reading.
  • Not filling the jet space of the burette: The space below the tap needs to be full, otherwise the titre will be recorded as larger than it actually is.
  • Reading the burette incorrectly: Always read from the bottom of the meniscus at eye level to avoid parallax error.

Indicators for Titration

Wide range indicators like litmus are not suitable as they don't give a sharp colour change at the endpoint. Methyl orange and phenolphthalein are ideal.
